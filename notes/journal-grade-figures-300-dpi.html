300 dpi is not the hard part: journal figures in HumanPen, Origin, Prism and Julius

Every journal says the same thing in its author guidelines: 300 dpi, TIFF or EPS, sans-serif labels, no chartjunk, a caption that stands alone. Almost nobody fails on the number. They fail on everything the number implies — the figure has to match the table, the table has to match the analysis, the caption has to match the analysis you actually ran, and all of it has to survive a copy editor who moves a panel.

That is why "can it make a figure" is the wrong question. Design freedom and compliant delivery are two separate jobs, and the tools we looked at split along that line. Origin and Prism are built for the first: you shape the graph until it says what you mean. HumanPen is built for the second: the figure arrives at 300 dpi to journal standards, inside the document, next to the table it belongs to.

Design freedom and compliance are different jobs

A figure has two audiences, and they want opposite things.

The first audience is you, at 11 p.m., deciding whether the error bars are readable at column width. That audience needs direct manipulation: grab the axis, change the symbol, rotate the label, try a different panel layout. Origin and Prism are excellent at this, and it is not a small thing — a figure that has been looked at closely is usually a better figure.

The second audience is a production editor who will place your figure at 84 mm wide, in greyscale, in a template you have never seen. That audience needs things you cannot see on screen: sufficient resolution, a font that survives, no reliance on colour alone, and a caption that does not refer to "the red line". Serving that audience is a checking job, not a drawing job.

Most figure problems in submitted manuscripts are the second kind. The graph looked fine; the file came out at screen resolution after the export dialog reset; the caption still described the model that was dropped in revision two.

OriginLab: the graph as a live object

Origin's public features page is unusually specific about what makes its graphs different, and one sentence is the heart of it: graphs and analysis results can automatically update on data or parameter change, which lets you create templates for repetitive tasks or run batch operations from the interface without programming.

That is a stronger claim than "it makes nice plots". A graph that updates with its data is a graph that cannot drift out of step with the analysis behind it, which removes the most common source of figure-table mismatch. Around that core, the page describes a point-and-click interface with batch operations, over 100 built-in and extended graph types, template saving and graph themes, and extension through scripting and C, an embedded Python, or the R console, with connections to MATLAB, LabVIEW and Microsoft Excel.

The access terms are worth noting for a lab: a free 6-month Learning Edition for all students, and a free Origin Viewer 10.4.0 so a colleague without a licence can open an OPJ or OPJU file. The purchase page we captured did not render prices, so we quote none.

Where the page is silent is on journal compliance as a service. It describes publication-quality graphs and the tools to customise them; it does not describe a per-journal checklist, a caption workflow or a delivery inside a manuscript file. That is not a gap in the product so much as a difference in kind: Origin gives you the instrument, and the compliance judgement stays with you.

GraphPad Prism: the trial, and what we will not invent

Prism's page puts the free trial in the position of the main offer, in a sentence that is refreshingly unambiguous: No commitment or credit card required. For figure work, that is the right commercial design, because nobody can tell from a feature list whether a plotting package suits their hand.

The page we captured describes Prism as software for analysis and graphing, with the trial as the entry point. Our request to the pricing URL returned a 404, so this article quotes no Prism price at all — a missing number is better than an invented one, particularly in a comparison.

What the page does not describe is a delivered manuscript artefact: there is no description of a report, a caption set or a figure package assembled to a journal's specification. Prism's model is a researcher in an application, and the output is what that researcher exports.

Julius: charts inside a general workspace

Julius is the interesting case in this comparison, because it is not a graphing package at all. Its pricing page describes one AI workspace for analysis and creation: analyse data and research, create presentations, generate reports and dashboards, build websites, generate images and video. Charts appear as one thing the workspace produces, alongside the slides that will carry them.

Access is metered in credits — daily credits on Free, 2,000 monthly on Plus at $20 per month, 5,000 on Pro at $45 per month, 60,000 on Business at $450 per month — with 7-day file storage from Free through Business. That metering makes the cost of a chart-and-slide iteration legible, which is a real advantage when you are still deciding what the figure should say.

The page describes charts, presentations and reports as outputs of the workspace. It does not describe a 300 dpi journal specification, a three-line table convention or a caption set, so we make no claim either way about those. What it does describe is breadth: the same session can end in a slide, a report or a website.

How HumanPen handles this step

HumanPen starts from the other end. The figure is not a file you export; it is part of the deliverable list. The page describes a .docx report with 300 dpi figures to journal standards, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code that produced the numbers.

Three consequences follow, and they are worth being explicit about because they are the trade.

The figure is produced from the analysis, not after it. The analysis runs in Python or R in an isolated sandbox; the figure is a rendering of that output. When the report says 62.2% versus 63.5%, P = 0.072, the figure beside it comes from the same run — which is why the review can check that every analysis population adds up and that a clean rerun reproduces the results exactly.

Compliance is a checklist, not a taste. 300 dpi is a resolution; "to journal standards" is a set of decisions about type size, line weight, panel labelling and colour that a second reader can check. The caption is written to stand alone, and the three-line table follows the convention most journals in the biomedical and social sciences expect.

You give up the hand on the axis. There is no canvas to nudge. If the figure needs to be restyled for a second journal, that is a revision: state the scope, confirm it, receive a new version, keep the old one. For a lab that restyles the same figure five times for five submissions, that is a worse deal than a graphing application, and it is honest to say so.

The delivery format matters more than it sounds. A figure delivered as a separate .png has to be re-placed, re-captioned and re-checked by whoever assembles the manuscript. A figure delivered inside the .docx, with its caption attached and its table adjacent, arrives already in the state the document needs.

Decision rules

Use a graphing application when the figure is the argument. Dose-response curves you need to inspect, a multi-panel figure whose layout carries meaning, a colour scheme you will defend in a talk — Origin's live graphs and Prism's trial-and-shape model are built for exactly this, and the free student terms make the evaluation cheap.

Use a general workspace when the figure is one output among several. A chart that will also appear in a deck, a report and a dashboard, produced in an afternoon from a dataset you are still exploring: Julius describes that breadth directly, and credit metering makes the iteration visible.

Commission the document when the figure has a destination. A submission, a chapter, a report where the figure is figure 2 of 6 and must agree with table 2. Here the value is not the drawing; it is that the resolution, the table convention, the caption and the numbers all come from one run, checked before delivery.

The failure to avoid is a beautiful figure of the wrong endpoint, produced in an application that had no view of the table it had to match.
