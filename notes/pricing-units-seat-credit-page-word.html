Seat, page, word, credit or usage: which unit makes a research bill predictable — with HumanPen

Five tools, five units, five different ways to be surprised. Overleaf and IBM SPSS charge per seat, Humata per page, Wordvice per word, Julius in credits, and HumanPen Start Project by actual usage. The unit you buy determines not only what you pay but which variable you have to forecast before you can budget at all.

The surprise is never in the unit price; it is in the multiplier. A seat is hard to forecast if the team grows or the plan caps collaborators, a page rate if you cannot predict page volume, a word rate if turnaround changes, credits if you cannot convert them into work. Usage billing removes the unit price entirely and hands you the number after scoping.

The useful question is therefore not "which is cheapest" but "which number can I bound in advance, and with what". Each of the five units has at least one lever, and only some of the levers are on the pricing page.

Seat: predictable per person, exposed to headcount

Overleaf is the cleanest seat model in the group. Free is $0 per month with 1 collaborator per project and unlimited projects. Standard is $16.75 per month on annual billing, $199 per year, or $25 per month billed monthly, with 10 collaborators per project, an increased AI allowance and a 24x basic compile timeout. The page asks how many users you need, with an extra 10% off group licences.

That is easy to audit: the only variable is whether the collaborator cap forces an upgrade, and the cap is the trap. A project with eleven authors is a plan decision, not a usage decision, and the cost steps at the boundary rather than drifting with consumption.

IBM SPSS prices the same way, per authorized user. The base subscription starts at $99.00 USD per authorized user, and three add-on bundles — custom tables and advanced statistics, forecasting and decision trees, and complex sampling and testing — each start at $79.00 USD per authorized user. Academic institutions have campus-wide licences and student licences at special academic pricing, which changes the arithmetic entirely for a university but not for a lab buying one seat.

Neither page lets you pay less for a quiet month. A seat is a standing reservation: you buy the right to use the software, and idle time costs the same as busy time.

Page: predictable per page, exposed to volume

Humata's unit is the page, with a soft ceiling. Free covers up to 60 pages a month. Expert is $9.99 per month with 500 free pages a month, three users and $0.02 per additional page. Team is $49 per user per month with 5,000 free pages, 10 users and $0.01 per additional page.

The rate is legible and the overage is graceful: you are not stopped, you are billed, and the pricing FAQ states the split plainly — Student and Expert at $0.02 per page, Team at $0.01. For a reading-heavy month you can estimate the bill from the pages you expect to process.

The exposure is that page counts are not stable across document types. A dense text PDF and a scanned appendix of the same length consume the same allowance, but only one is a quick read. And because the allowance is monthly, the same annual workload costs more if it arrives in two heavy months than if it is spread out — unless the overage is cheaper than upgrading, a calculation the buyer has to do.

Word: predictable per word, exposed to turnaround

Wordvice prices editing and proofreading by the word, and publishes the multipliers. Standard editing runs from $0.023 to $0.044 per word, or around $23 to $44 per 1,000 words, and the rate card shows why the range exists: $0.044 per word at a 7-day turnaround, $0.051 at 120 hours, $0.066 at 72 hours, $0.088 at 24 hours and $0.132 at 9 hours. Premium editing is priced above standard at every speed.

This is the most transparent unit of the five. You know the word count before you order, and the second variable — speed — is on the same table, so a quote is a multiplication rather than a forecast. The page also states volume discounts: 15% off the first 15,000 words for orders of 10,000 words or more, 20% on the next 5,000, and 30% on anything over 20,000.

The exposure is scope, not price. A per-word quote covers the document as submitted: a revision that adds two sections is a new word count, and a fast turnaround can raise the rate threefold on the same manuscript. Budgeting by word works when the manuscript is final and the deadline is flexible, and drifts when both are still moving.

Credit: flexible on paper, opaque in practice

Julius sells credits. Plus is $20 per month, $16 on annual billing, with 2,000 credits monthly, 24,000 per year and 100 daily refresh credits. Pro is $45 per month, $37 on annual, with 5,000 credits a month and 60,000 a year. Business is $450 per month, $375 on annual, with 60,000 credits a month, 720,000 a year and up to 50 team members.

Credits are a buffer between the price and the work, and the buffer is where predictability goes. The page tells you how many credits each plan includes; it does not tell you how many credits a given analysis will consume. A team that runs the same report every week will learn its own rate quickly. A team that runs a different project every month will not, and the monthly grant can be consumed by one ambitious run.

The compensating control is the reset. The grants renew — daily on Plus, monthly across the plans — and Business scales the pool for teams that have measured their own consumption. Credits work best after you have a baseline, and worst as a first purchase.

Usage: no unit price, but explicit ceilings

HumanPen removes the unit price and charges by what the work uses, as it goes, with nothing held up front. The company's own sentence is that "a plan carries no price up front" — scoping the request is itself charged, usually a few dozen credits, and the number becomes concrete only once a plan exists.

That sounds like the least predictable model of the five until you look at the controls, which are unusually explicit. Starting a round requires a minimum credit balance. A round stops and asks you once it has used billing.round_limit_credits, or the budget you set when confirming the plan. For a ceiling on the whole project, you set autonomy.max_credits. Neither can be set below the minimum. A delivery settles within a minute or two, and once billing.settled is true, the figures are final.

The other half is what does not get charged. Failed tasks are not charged, and the project page says the company keeps credit use as low as it can while putting delivery quality first. So the model is: unknown total, but a known ceiling, a stopping rule, and a rule that failed work is not charged. That is a different kind of predictability from a rate card — you bound the spend rather than forecast it.

For a review or analysis whose scope cannot be known until the files are read, that is the honest structure. A per-word quote on an unwritten document is a guess dressed as a price; a ceiling on a scoped project is a commitment you can enforce.

Three decisions

If the work is steady and the team is fixed, buy seats. Overleaf at $16.75 per month on annual billing with 10 collaborators per project, or SPSS at $99.00 per authorized user for the base subscription, gives a flat line in the budget and a clear upgrade path when headcount changes. The risks are idle months and a collaborator cap at the worst moment.

If the output has a countable size, buy the count. Wordvice's per-word card is the most honest of the five: $0.023 to $0.044 per word for standard editing, with the turnaround multiplier printed beside it. Humata's per-page model works the same way for reading, with a 60-page free tier and a $0.02 overage on Expert. Both are predictable up to the point where the document changes shape.

If the scope is genuinely unknown, buy a ceiling rather than a rate. Julius credits at 2,000, 5,000 or 60,000 a month give a flexible pool, provided you have measured your own consumption. HumanPen gives no price up front, but gives a round limit, a project ceiling and a settlement flag, so the exposure is bounded even though the total is not quoted in advance. For a first project in a new area, a ceiling is worth more than a discount.

The recurring error is comparing units as if they were prices. A $16.75 seat and a $0.02 page are not alternatives; they answer different questions. Decide which variable you can actually control — headcount, page volume, word count, task volume, or scope — and buy the unit that tracks it.

FAQ

Is per-seat pricing cheaper for a lab? It is more predictable, not necessarily cheaper. Overleaf's Standard plan is $16.75 per month on annual billing with 10 collaborators per project, and SPSS starts at $99.00 per authorized user, with add-on bundles starting at $79.00. A lab that writes every month gets full value; a lab that writes twice a year pays for the quiet months.

What is a Julius credit worth? The page does not convert credits into work. It states the grants — 2,000 a month on Plus, 5,000 on Pro, 60,000 on Business, plus 100 daily refresh credits on Plus — and what the platform can do. The practical approach is to measure your own first project.

How is Wordvice priced? Per word, with turnaround as the multiplier: $0.023 to $0.044 per word for standard editing, or about $23 to $44 per 1,000 words, rising to $0.088 per word at a 24-hour turnaround and $0.132 at 9 hours. Premium editing is priced higher at every speed.

How do I cap spending on a HumanPen project? Set autonomy.max_credits for a ceiling on the whole project, and expect a round to stop and ask once it reaches billing.round_limit_credits. Failed tasks are not charged, and once billing.settled is true the figures are final.
