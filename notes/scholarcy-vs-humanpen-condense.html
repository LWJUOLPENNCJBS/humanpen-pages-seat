Scholarcy vs HumanPen Condense: Summary Flashcards vs a Shorter Draft of Your Own File

Short answer

Choose Scholarcy when the reading is the problem and not the writing. Its public page describes summarising any paper, article or textbook — videos included — and turning long texts into interactive "summary flashcards" that highlight key information. It takes PDFs, book chapters, articles, studies, plain text, Zotero libraries, Google Drive files and YouTube links, and the page states "Join over 600,000 people saving time". That is an intake system for other people's work, built for the academic habit of reading more than you can hold.

Choose HumanPen Condense when the document is already yours and its length is now the problem: a chapter that has to fit a word limit, a grant narrative that must lose paragraphs without losing numbers, a lit review whose citations cannot drift. Condense reduces a document to a target word count while preserving meaning, structure and citations: an overall word budget, or a separate target per selected passage. It keeps facts, numbers, citations and structure and trims wordiness and repetition; a .docx goes in, and an editable DOCX with the original styles and layout comes back, at 20 credits per 1,000 words.

The distinction that matters is what the output is. A summariser replaces your document with a shorter reading of it — a digest, a set of flashcards, something to skim. Condense returns your document at a shorter length, for a word limit someone else set. One is a reading aid, the other a deliverable; nothing here says one is better at being the other.

Where the two tools actually diverge

1. A digest is a new document; a condense is your document

Flashcards are a separate artefact. They sit beside the paper, highlight what a summariser judged key, and let you skip the source. That is a good purpose, and a different one from having a manuscript come back at the length the journal asked for.

The consequence is practical: a digest cannot be submitted. If your supervisor asks for the same chapter, shorter, flashcards do not answer the request; the chapter, edited, its argument order intact and citation numbering unbroken, does.

HumanPen Condense is scoped to that request only. The page does not describe reading papers for you and does not offer a digest; it describes one operation with a number attached — reduce to a target, keep what must be kept, hand the file back.

2. One tool chooses what matters; the other is told what to keep

Every summariser makes an editorial decision for you: this sentence is key, that paragraph is not. That is what "highlight key information" means — efficient when you are triaging literature, costly when the dropped sentence carries your sample size or your ethics approval number.

Condense inverts the decision. The page states it keeps facts, numbers, citations and structure, and trims wordiness and repetition — compression from phrasing, not from the evidence. And because the budget can be set per selected passage, you can hold the methods section near its original length while cutting the discussion, instead of applying one ratio to sections that do not deserve equal treatment.

Neither approach is superior: a summariser built for skimming has no reason to preserve a citation, and a reducer built for a word limit has every reason to.

3. The academic intake on one side, the file contract on the other

Scholarcy's page is broad on intake: PDFs, book chapters, articles, studies, plain text, Zotero, Google Drive, YouTube. That breadth is the product — researchers live in those sources, and pulling a Zotero library or a Drive folder into a summary flow removes real friction.

Condense's intake is deliberately narrow: .docx only. The compensating promise is on the output side — the same editable DOCX, original styles and layout intact, so the document still looks like itself after the edit. If your source is a scanned PDF or a lecture video, draft first, then condense the draft.

That is the honest trade: broad reading intake versus a strict file contract on the way out.

When to choose which (or use both)

Choose Scholarcy if your problem is the pile: a reading list you cannot finish, a literature scan before you commit to a topic, a textbook chapter you need to extract from rather than rewrite. Choose it too if your inputs are mostly PDFs, video or library exports rather than documents you authored.

Choose HumanPen Condense if the document exists and the limit is fixed: an abstract over the cap, a section a co-author wants halved, a report that must land inside a page count without dropping a figure. Choose it when the output must be openable and editable by someone else.

Use both, in order. Skim with a summariser to decide what to read; write from what survives; then condense your own draft to the length the venue enforces. The steps do not overlap.

It does not describe editing your own DOCX down to a specific target word count while keeping your citations and structure in place.
