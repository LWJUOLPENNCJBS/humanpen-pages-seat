ResearchRabbit vs HumanPen Start Project: Citation-Graph Discovery vs Executed Analysis

Short answer

Choose ResearchRabbit when you are still deciding what to read. It takes up to 50 seed articles on the free tier, or 300 on RR+, and turns them into a map of the literature you can walk outward from. The free tier is unusually generous for that job: unlimited searches across 310+ million articles, unlimited library and collections, and collections you can share.

Choose HumanPen Start Project when you have already stopped reading and started measuring. You attach Excel, CSV, Word, PDF or zip up to 5 GB total, confirm a plan, Python or R runs in an isolated sandbox, and what comes back is a .docx report with three-line tables, 300 dpi figures, a .pptx with speaker notes and the code — after a second AI has reviewed it.

One builds the map of a field. The other produces the number that goes into the paper. Both are necessary, and neither substitutes for the other.

Where the workflows actually diverge

1. Fifty seeds or a hundred columns

ResearchRabbit's interface is a graph grown from seeds. The free tier allows up to 50 seed articles; RR+ raises it to 300 "for large scale reviews" and adds advanced search controls and multiple projects so one topic does not contaminate another. The work is iterative: you add a paper you like, the graph re-centres, you follow a co-author, you notice a cluster you had missed.

Start Project has no graph and no seeds. It has a file and a brief. The page's own framing is unusually concrete about the intake: drop in data, papers or a zip file, and the system confirms requirements before anything runs. The five stages — Describe, Plan, In progress, In review, Delivered — are a straight line, not a walk.

If your uncertainty is "what exists", the graph is the better instrument. If your uncertainty is "what does my data show", walking a graph will not resolve it.

2. Discovery versus estimation

ResearchRabbit answers a question about provenance: which papers sit near this one, who else writes in this vein, what is adjacent. Its home page puts it plainly — the product "analyzes papers you love and finds the research you've been missing".

Start Project answers a question about magnitude. The published example is a secondary analysis of the International Stroke Trial: 19,435 patients in the full analysis set, primary endpoint death or dependency at 6 months, about 150 missing outcomes handled with a sensitivity analysis, aspirin group 62.2% against control 63.5%, OR 0.95 (0.89–1.00), P = 0.072. The review panel checks that the analysis populations add up, that a clean rerun reproduces the results exactly, and that every reference resolves.

Provenance and magnitude are different kinds of knowledge. A well-mapped field still does not tell you the odds ratio in your own cohort.

3. Where the tools touch

They do meet in one place: the reading list. ResearchRabbit exports a curated collection — the papers you decided matter. Those papers, plus your dataset, are exactly the sort of thing Start Project accepts, since PDF and zip are both on its intake list and the page lists literature review and evidence building as a use case, with a PubMed search and every DOI checked.

The handoff is the interesting part of a real workflow. Discovery produces a set; estimation consumes a set plus a table. The failure mode is trying to make the discovery tool produce the table, or handing a graph screenshot to a statistician and calling it a literature review.

Uploads and what you actually receive

ResearchRabbit's unit of storage is the collection. The free tier gives unlimited library and collections, sharing, and 50 seeds; RR+ adds multiple projects and Signals alerts. Institutions get LibKey integration so the collection connects to what the library already licenses, plus volume discounts and user management. That is a well-shaped product for a reading workflow, and the free tier is genuinely usable rather than a demo.

Start Project's unit of storage is the project folder. Intake is up to 5 GB total across Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip; Wind or CNKI data must be exported first, and GPU training is not yet supported. Output is the report, the figures, the tables, the slides with speaker notes, and the code. Revisions are requested in the project, the scope is explained before work starts, a new version is delivered and older versions are kept.

Note what is absent from the second list: any notion of a shared reading list. Start Project is not where you keep your library. It is where you spend it.

Pricing and governance

ResearchRabbit's ladder is short. Free Forever is $0 with unlimited searches across 310+ million articles, unlimited library and collections, collection sharing and up to 50 seed articles. RR+ is $10 per month on the annual plan or $12.5 per month on the monthly plan, adding 300 seed articles, advanced search controls, multiple projects and Signals alerts. Institution is priced on contact, with LibKey integration, volume discounts and user management for thousands of users.

Start Project has no seat or message allowance to stand against it. Work is scoped and confirmed before it runs, so "how much per month" does not map cleanly onto it, and we are not going to manufacture a comparison between a subscription and a project.

Governance is where the two pages differ most in what they publish. ResearchRabbit's home and pricing pages do not describe data handling, training policy or retention, so this article asserts nothing about them. Start Project states its positions on the page: encrypted in transit, an isolated sandbox per project, no sharing across users, no use for training, deletion at any time, and 6 months of retention after the last activity with a reminder 3 days before expiry. If you are writing a data-management paragraph for a grant, only one of these two gives you sentences to quote today.

When to choose which

Choose ResearchRabbit if:

  • You are entering a field and need to see its shape before you commit to a question.
  • You want a free tier that actually works: unlimited searches, unlimited collections, sharing, 50 seeds.
  • You need alerts when new work appears in your area, which Signals provides on RR+.
  • Your institution licenses content through LibKey and you want the discovery layer to meet the library where it is.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if:

  • You have the data and the reading is done; the missing step is the statistics.
  • The analysis was done in SPSS or Stata and needs redoing in Python or R with the code kept.
  • The deliverable is a report with three-line tables, 300 dpi figures, a narrative that keeps every number and citation intact, and slides for the group meeting.
  • You need revisions versioned rather than a fresh conversation each round.

Use both if your project runs discovery then estimation. Grow the collection in ResearchRabbit until the gap is visible and the key papers are pinned, export the list, then start a project with those PDFs plus your dataset and let Python or R produce the tables and figures. The literature half finishes with a set of papers; the analysis half finishes with a report you can defend line by line. Trying to do both in one tool is where most literature-based empirical projects lose a month.

FAQ

Is ResearchRabbit free? Yes — "Free Forever" at $0, with unlimited searches across 310+ million articles, unlimited library and collections, collection sharing and up to 50 seed articles.

What does RR+ add over the free tier? For $10 per month on the annual plan, or $12.5 per month on the monthly plan: 300 seed articles for large-scale reviews, advanced search controls, multiple projects and Signals alerts.

Does ResearchRabbit run statistical analysis on my data? The public pages we examined describe discovery and organisation — mapping connections between papers, building collections, alerts. They do not describe a statistical analysis execution environment, so a regression or a survival analysis is not something to expect from it.

How many papers does ResearchRabbit cover? The pricing page says "Unlimited searches across 310+ million articles"; the home page says "Access over 320 million academic papers". Both figures were on the site on the same day, which is worth knowing if you are citing the number in a methods paragraph.
