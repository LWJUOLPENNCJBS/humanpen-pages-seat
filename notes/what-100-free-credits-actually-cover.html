What the 100 free HumanPen credits actually cover, and what they do not

A free balance is useful only if you know its shape. HumanPen offers one hundred free credits on registration, and the public pages state the rules that determine how far that balance goes. This page collects those rules, gives two bounded examples of what to try first, and names the things a free balance is not — because "free credits" is often read as "a free plan".

The key distinction is between a balance and a guarantee. One hundred credits is a starting amount. What it buys depends on the volume actually rewritten in a document job, or on the usage of a confirmed project. The pages state the rate for one unit of humanizing and the rules for charging; they do not state how far any particular balance will stretch on your file.

Two rules shape a first experiment. Charging only for words rewritten means a report-targeted pass on a small flagged set is a small job. And failed tasks are not charged, which means the downside of trying a bounded first job is limited.

Example one: a short flagged document

The most controlled way to use the starting balance is a document you can inspect end to end. Upload a short DOCX, import a Turnitin or iThenticate report if you have one, and let the tool target flagged passages. The public page states that one thousand words costs one hundred credits and that you are charged only for the words actually rewritten. A short flagged set is therefore well inside a first balance, though the exact figure depends on your file.

What comes back is the same file type, editable, with styling untouched, tables and numbering in place, and numbers, units, formulas, citations, terminology and claim strength preserved. Read the changed paragraphs and confirm the scientific content did not move. If a later report still flags passages at or above the stated threshold, the documented workflow lets you import it and have the remaining passages rewritten for free.

This example is a measurement. After it, you know the rate as it applies to a real file of yours, which is more useful than any estimated page count.

Example two: a small project with a visible plan

A project is a different use of the balance. Describe a bounded task and attach a small dataset, then read the plan before confirming. The developer page says scoping is charged, usually a few dozen credits, and that a plan carries no price up front. Credits are charged by usage as the work runs, and a plan can set a budget for a round and a maximum for the whole project.

A sensible first project is small enough that the plan is easy to read: one table to rebuild, one figure to regenerate, one short report. Confirm the ceiling you are comfortable with, and remember that failed tasks are not charged. After delivery, check the settled flag before treating the usage figures as final.

Note what this example is not. It is not a free analysis of a large dataset, and it is not an open-ended research assistant. It is a bounded job whose scope you can see before it starts.

What the balance does not buy

It does not buy a subscription. One hundred credits is a registration offer, and the home page mentions a first-order discount next to it; the balance is finite. When it runs out, the projects FAQ says you need to top up to continue.

It does not buy an unlimited custom analysis. GPU training is not supported, Wind and CNKI data must be exported first, and analyses originally done in SPSS or Stata are performed in Python or R. If your first idea depends on one of those, choose a different first task.

It does not buy permanent storage. Projects are kept for six months after the last activity with a reminder, and can be deleted at any time. Export your deliverable to your own repository.

It does not buy a detector outcome. The documented re-check workflow describes targeted rewrites and free rounds on still-flagged passages. It does not promise a score, and the protected-elements section says deliberate typos are not introduced.

Why the rate is a better guide than a page count

People often ask how many pages one hundred credits will produce. That question cannot be answered honestly from the public pages, because the charge follows words rewritten, not pages delivered. A document with a wide margin and short paragraphs has few words on many pages. A dense results chapter has many words on a few pages. The same balance behaves differently on the two.

The rate is the stable quantity. One thousand words costs one hundred credits, and the charge is for words actually rewritten. If you want to predict a first job, count the words in the passages you expect to change, not the length of the file. A report-targeted pass makes that count smaller still, because untouched paragraphs are not rewritten and their layout is left alone.

For a project, substitute the plan for the word count. A plan that names one table, one figure and a short report is a bounded piece of work whose usage you can check against the ceiling you set. A plan that says "analyse the dataset" is not bounded, and a first balance is the wrong place to discover what that means.

Keeping the experiment honest

Write two things down before you spend the balance: what you expect the job to produce, and what you expect it to cost. The first is a plan or a flagged passage list. The second is at most an expectation based on the stated rate, since no total is quoted. After delivery, compare both. If the output matched the expectation, you have learned that the workflow fits your task. If it did not, the free balance has cost you nothing beyond the time spent describing the task carefully.

Keep the input file and the returned file together, even for a small test. A first job is most useful as a reference point for later ones: when a colleague asks whether a rewritten chapter kept its tables, you can answer from your own document rather than from a marketing page.

How to decide between the two examples

Choose the document example if you already have a manuscript with flagged passages and want to understand the rewrite workflow. Choose the project example if your problem is an analysis or a report that does not exist yet. Do not try to do both in one first job: a project that also needs document passes is two tasks, and a first balance is easier to learn from when the task is single.

Whichever you choose, write down what you expected before you start and compare it with the actual usage afterwards. That habit is the only reliable way to turn a free balance into a decision rather than an anecdote.

Frequently asked questions

Is one hundred credits a recurring allowance?

No. It is described as a registration offer. It is finite, and the projects FAQ says a balance must be topped up to continue once it runs out.

Does a full document of one thousand words cost exactly one hundred credits?

Only if all one thousand words are rewritten. The rule is that you are charged for words actually rewritten, so a targeted pass on fewer words costs less.

Is the re-check round free?

The documented workflow states that if a later report still shows passages flagged at or above the threshold, you import it and the remaining passages are rewritten at no extra credits.

Is confirming a project plan free?

No. Scoping is charged, usually a few dozen credits. The plan itself carries no price up front.

Are there file limits on a first upload?

Document jobs accept DOCX, PPTX and TXT with no word limit. Research uploads accept Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip files up to five gigabytes in total, with Wind or CNKI exported first.
