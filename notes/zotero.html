Zotero vs HumanPen Start Project: Who Owns the Library, and Who Reads It

Zotero is the right choice when the library itself is the asset. It is free, easy to use, and built for collecting, organising, annotating, citing and sharing research; the page says you can share a Zotero library with as many people as you like, at no cost. Storage is the paid part: 300 MB free, then 2 GB at $20/year, 6 GB at $60/year, and a higher tier at $120/year. What you end up owning is a library that stays yours and moves with you.

HumanPen Start Project is the right choice when the library is an input rather than the output. You hand over a manuscript or a question, the project returns a plan, and on confirmation it reads the paper, runs its own literature search, and delivers a review .docx with an evidence table, numbers kept exactly as they are, and a reference check that reports all 11 references found, one by one. What you end up owning is a document with a defensible bibliography.

The distinction is custody. Zotero answers "where do my references live, and who can see them?" HumanPen answers "what does the literature actually say, and can someone audit the claim?" Most research groups need both answers, and they are not in competition.

A library is a possession; a review is a reading

Zotero's design starts from accumulation: collect items, sort them into collections, tag them, annotate the PDFs, cite from them in whatever style you need. The value compounds — a library built over a PhD is worth more in year five than in year one — and the page's promise that you can share it with as many people as you like, at no cost, is a promise about custody rather than output.

That makes storage the only commercial question Zotero has to ask. The software is free; what costs money is where the PDFs live. 300 MB free covers a reading list, 2 GB at $20/year a working researcher, 6 GB at $60/year a lab, and $120/year a group that has stopped counting. None of those tiers changes what the tool does — only how much of it you can carry.

HumanPen's design starts from reading rather than keeping. The deliverable is a document, and the literature work is instrumental to it: a review that states what the evidence shows, with a table that lays the studies side by side and a reference list that has been checked rather than assembled. The public example goes further than a bibliography — a PubMed search with each DOI verified individually, and a review step that reports "all 11 references found, one by one" instead of assuming that a citation that parses is a citation that exists.

Put crudely: Zotero is where your papers live, HumanPen is where your argument is made. A researcher who owns a very large PDF library and no draft has a library, not a paper.

What "sharing" means in each

Zotero's sharing is lateral. A shared library is a shared possession: co-authors see the same items, tags and annotations, and the bibliography stays consistent because everyone cites from one pool. Nothing in that arrangement is about who writes what.

HumanPen's sharing is vertical, because the work is commissioned. The plan is the shared artefact — population, sources, what will be checked, what the deliverable contains — and it is versioned, so a change of mind is a new version rather than an edit nobody notices.

The two coexist without friction. A shared library gives a group a record of what has been read; a commissioned review says what the record means. The first is a filing system with good manners, the second an argument with a paper trail.

Where the references get checked

This is the part most reference managers leave to you.

Review before delivery has four named checks, one of them bibliographic: all 11 references found, one by one. The others concern the analysis — the primary endpoint matching at 62.2% against 63.5% with P = 0.072, every analysis population adding up, a clean rerun reproducing the numbers. In the public example the reference work is a PubMed search with each DOI checked against the record.

Zotero's contribution to that problem is structural rather than editorial: if an item is in your library, its metadata came from somewhere and can be inspected; if it is not, you did not cite it. That is a strong safeguard against invented references, and it is the reason reference managers exist. What the storage and home pages we examined describe is collection, organisation, annotation, citation and sharing — they do not describe an agent that reads a manuscript, searches the literature and drafts the review.

So they address different failure modes. Zotero prevents the failure where you cite something you never read; the review step addresses the failure where a citation does not say what the sentence claims — which is why the check is per reference rather than per bibliography.

Uploads and what lands on your desk

Zotero's unit of exchange is the item. You capture a reference, attach the PDF, annotate it, and the library grows. There is no upload envelope in the project sense; there is a storage allowance, and the tiers above describe it.

HumanPen's unit of exchange is a delivery. The intake is stated precisely: Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip, up to 5 GB total — enough for a manuscript, a protocol, a dataset and reviewer comments in one place. Analysis runs in Python or R; work that began in SPSS or Stata is redone in Python or R; Wind and CNKI data must be exported first; GPU training is not supported.

The outputs are the difference in kind. From Zotero you get a formatted bibliography and a library you can keep citing from. From the project you get a .docx report, charts at 300 dpi, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes and the code. The writing step has a constraint that matters for a review: a de-AI-tone pass that leaves numbers and citations exactly as they are, shown in a public before-and-after that keeps the same percentages and the same P value.

For a submission, the .docx is a draft you then style; for a shared bibliography, the library is the artefact.

Price and governance

Zotero's commercial model is clean: the tool is free, and the only thing sold is storage — 300 MB free, 2 GB at $20/year, 6 GB at $60/year, $120/year for the higher tier. The tiers scale with the size of a library, not the number of people using it, which fits the page's statement that a library can be shared at no cost.

HumanPen does not sell seats or storage. Billing is metered by actual usage, failed tasks are not billed, and the plan carries no price up front — scoping typically costs tens of credits, and the figure becomes concrete once a plan exists. A round limit stops the project and asks before continuing; a per-project credit ceiling caps exposure. When billing.settled is true, the number is final.

Governance makes the custody question practical. Zotero's library is yours in the plainest sense: items live in your account, the tool is free, and the storage tiers are hosting rather than lock-in. HumanPen's page commits to encryption in transit, an isolated sandbox per project, no sharing across users, no use for training, deletion at any time, and retention of six months after the last activity with a reminder three days before expiry.

For a review built on published papers, the first model is enough — the material is public. For a review built on an unpublished manuscript plus a dataset, the second set of commitments is the one a supervisor will ask about, and the per-project sandbox is the part that makes the answer short.

When to choose which

Choose Zotero if you are building a body of literature you intend to keep. Free at the tool level, sharing at no cost, and storage that starts at 300 MB free before the 2 GB, 6 GB and $120/year tiers — it is the cheapest way to own a citation record that survives a change of institution, a change of laptop and a change of topic.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if the deliverable is a review document rather than a library. A literature review chapter, a background section that has to survive a reviewer, an evidence table for a grant — these need reading, synthesis, a reference check and a formatted .docx, and the plan-first structure means you approve the scope before the searching starts. The 5 GB intake also means the manuscript you are extending can travel with the brief.

Use both if you want a library you own and a review you can defend. Keep the library as the record of what has been collected; commission the review as a document that reads it, searches beyond it and returns an evidence table with every reference accounted for. Then file the deliverable back into the library.

The mistake to avoid is treating a large library as a literature review. A library is a list of things that exist; a review is a claim about what they show, and the distance between the two is measured in reading.

FAQ

Is Zotero free? Yes. The tool is free and easy to use, and the page states that a library can be shared with as many people as you like, at no cost. Storage is the paid part.

How much does Zotero storage cost? 300 MB is free, 2 GB is $20/year, 6 GB is $60/year, and a higher tier is $120/year. The storage page also gives monthly equivalents for the paid tiers.

Can Zotero write my literature review? The pages we examined describe collecting, organising, annotating, citing and sharing research. They do not describe drafting a review from a manuscript, so we make no claim either way. A HumanPen project takes the brief and the files, returns a plan, and delivers a review .docx with an evidence table and a reference check reporting all 11 references found, one by one.

Do I still need Zotero if I use HumanPen for the review? Yes, if you want the library to remain yours. The project delivers a document and the code behind it; the library is a separate asset that you keep, share at no cost and carry between institutions.
