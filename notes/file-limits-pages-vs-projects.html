Pages, megabytes or one total: what an upload limit actually lets you hand over — Humata, AskYourPDF, Unriddle, Zotero and HumanPen

Every document tool advertises a limit, and almost none of them mean the same thing. Humata counts pages per month. AskYourPDF caps pages and megabytes per document. Unriddle caps both for a single upload. Zotero sells a pool of storage that persists. HumanPen Start Project states one number for a whole project — Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip up to 5 GB total — and describes no per-file page ceiling.

That difference decides what you can realistically hand over in one go. The same 300-page scanned PDF is one document at AskYourPDF, 300 pages of a monthly allowance at Humata, part of a 10,000-page upload at Unriddle's Pro tier, an entry in your Zotero storage, and a small fraction of a HumanPen project. The number on the pricing page is only meaningful once you know which of those it is.

The practical rule: a monthly page allowance suits steady reading, a per-document cap suits one big file, a storage pool suits a library you keep, and a single per-project total suits a mixed brief that has to arrive together.

Humata: a monthly page allowance

Humata's Free plan is described as basic features for up to 60 free pages of use, and the home page states the same allowance as 60 pages per month. That is a recurring budget, not a per-file limit: a single 200-page thesis does not fit on Free, however small the file.

Expert is $9.99 per month for moderate usage by small professional teams of up to three people, with up to 500 free pages a month, three users included, and $0.02 per additional page. Team is $49 per user per month for teams up to 10 people, with 5,000 free pages a month, 10 users included, and $0.01 per additional page.

The overage mechanism is the interesting part, because it makes the limit soft. You are not blocked at 500 pages; you are billed $0.02 for each page beyond it, and the pricing FAQ confirms that Student and Expert plans are charged at $0.02 per page while Team is charged at $0.01. For a group that reads in bursts, a soft limit is more useful than a hard one; for a group that wants a fixed bill, it is the opposite.

Note what the page does not say: it describes pages of uploaded PDFs and a monthly allowance, and it does not describe a maximum file size, a maximum page count per document, or a project-level total. If your constraint is one very large scanned file, that is a question the pricing page does not answer.

AskYourPDF: a cap per document

AskYourPDF prices in documents rather than months. Free allows up to 100 pages per document and a 15 MB maximum upload. Premium, at $11.99 per month billed yearly, allows up to 2,500 pages per document and 31 MB. Pro, at $14.99 per month billed yearly, allows up to 6,000 pages per document and 877 MB.

That shape suits the single large file. A 900-page regulatory dossier or a bundle of scanned appendices raises one question: does this document fit? The page's comparison table lists pages per document and max upload size side by side through Free, Premium, Pro and Enterprise, with Enterprise at 1,431 MB and unlimited pages per document.

The two caps bind differently. A text-dense 6,000-page PDF may pass the page test and still fail the megabyte test, or the reverse for a scanned document full of images. Because both thresholds are stated, you can check a specific file before buying. What the page describes is a per-document ceiling and a daily document count; it does not describe a project that accumulates files toward a single total.

Unriddle: megabytes or pages, per upload

Unriddle states its limit as a pair. Free covers 20 MB or 120 pages per upload. Plus, at $10 per seat per month billed yearly, covers 100 MB or 600 pages. Pro, at $20 per seat per month, covers 300 MB or 10,000 pages. Max is $100 per seat per month for 5x the Pro usage and $200 for 20x, and the same plans are $8, $16, $80 and $160 per seat per month on annual billing.

The "or" matters. A 10,000-page text PDF will usually blow the megabyte limit long before the page limit on the lower tiers, so the effective ceiling is the smaller of the two. On Pro, 300 MB is a substantial single upload, but still one upload at a time.

The pricing page also describes credits as a separate meter: included usage refreshes daily on Free and resets in full seven days after first use on paid plans, and owners can buy $5 to $1,000 of credits at a time, with web-purchased credits expiring 12 months after purchase. That second meter governs how much analysis the uploaded material can support.

Zotero: storage you keep

Zotero is the outlier, because its limit is about persistence rather than processing. Storage holds PDFs, images, web snapshots and other files, synchronises them across devices, shares attachments in group libraries and makes files available through the online library. The individual tiers are 300 MB free, a paid tier at $20 per year, 6 GB at $60 per year, and unlimited at $120 per year.

That is a pool, not a per-task budget. Uploading a paper does not consume an allowance that resets; it occupies space until you delete it. For a researcher building a library over years, that is exactly the right shape. For a researcher handing over 40 PDFs plus a dataset for a single analysis, it is the wrong shape — storage says nothing about what can be processed in one task.

The storage page also states that group libraries can use Zotero Storage at no additional charge, with group file storage always drawing from the storage account of the group owner. So the number you buy is a shared ceiling for the group, not a per-member allowance.

HumanPen: one total for the project

HumanPen states the intake as a single sentence: Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip up to 5 GB total. There is no monthly page allowance, no per-document page cap and no separate storage pool described on the page. The unit is the project, and the limit is the sum of everything you attach to it.

That is a deliberate match to the work. A review project does not receive one tidy PDF; it receives the manuscript being extended, the papers already trusted, a dataset in Excel or CSV, a target journal's template in Word, and often a zip of reviewer comments. Metering those separately would force a choice about which file matters, when the point is that they matter together. At 5 GB, the constraint is rarely the literature itself and more often a very large imaging or raw-data archive, which is a different problem.

The page also states what happens to the material: analysis runs on Python or R, work originally done in SPSS or Stata is redone in Python or R, Wind and CNKI data must be exported first, and GPU training is not supported. None of that is a quota, but it is the other half of "what can I hand over": a file format that the pipeline cannot read is a limit no pricing tier fixes.

Three decisions

If you have one enormous document, buy a per-document cap. AskYourPDF's ladder is explicit about both thresholds — 100 pages and 15 MB free, 2,500 pages and 31 MB at $11.99, 6,000 pages and 877 MB at $14.99 — so you can test the actual file against the actual numbers. Unriddle's Pro tier at 300 MB or 10,000 pages per upload is the alternative when the document is image-heavy.

If you read steadily across a term, buy a monthly allowance or a storage pool. Humata's 60 pages a month on Free and 500 or 5,000 on paid plans suit a reading rhythm, and the $0.02 or $0.01 overage means a heavy week does not stop the work. Zotero's 300 MB free and paid tiers from $20 per year suit the library you keep, where the question is what you can hold rather than what you can process this month.

If the brief is mixed, buy a project total. A manuscript, a dataset, a template and a folder of papers arriving together is a single 5 GB intake at HumanPen, with the plan confirmed before work starts. The advantage is not that 5 GB is larger than anyone else's number — the units are not comparable — but that there is one number to check instead of four allowances to reconcile across tools.

The trap is comparing a page allowance with a byte total and concluding one is bigger. Sixty pages a month and 5 GB per project measure different things: one is throughput over time, the other is the size of a single commission. Work out whether your constraint is recurring volume, one large file, or one mixed brief, and the pricing table stops being ambiguous.

FAQ

Is Humata's 60 pages a month or per file? Per month. The Free plan is described as basic features for up to 60 free pages of use, and the home page states 60 pages per month. Expert includes 500 free pages a month and Team 5,000, with $0.02 and $0.01 per additional page respectively.

What is the largest PDF AskYourPDF accepts? On the plans captured, Free allows 15 MB and up to 100 pages per document, Premium 31 MB and 2,500 pages, and Pro 877 MB and 6,000 pages. Enterprise is listed at 1,431 MB with unlimited pages per document.

Does Unriddle limit pages or megabytes? Both, per upload: 20 MB or 120 pages on Free, 100 MB or 600 pages on Plus, and 300 MB or 10,000 pages on Pro. Whichever ceiling is reached first ends the upload.

How much can I upload to a HumanPen project? Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip up to 5 GB total, across everything attached to the project. The page does not describe a separate per-file page limit, and it does describe the formats the analysis pipeline accepts.
