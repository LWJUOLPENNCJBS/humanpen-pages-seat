Before you choose an academic AI, ask these twelve questions — a HumanPen selection checklist

The fastest way to choose badly is to pick the tool with the prettiest demo; the second fastest is to pick on price per seat. This piece does neither. It gives you twelve questions whose answers are visible on a public page, and what a workable answer looks like.

The judgment up front: for a literature chat, most tools are adequate. For work that has to survive a methods section — a dataset uploaded once, an analysis someone else will rerun — the questions that matter are about inputs, code, verification, retention and billing. They appear below in the order a project hits them.

Editorial disclosure: this article is published by HumanPen. Statements about other products are based on the public pages we examined on 2026-11-24; no synthetic benchmark was run, and prices change, so confirm on the vendor's own page.

1. Is the plan fixed before any work starts?

Why ask. If a tool starts working the moment you press enter, you discover the scope after the cost. You want a plan you can read, correct and approve.

How to check. Look for a stated gate between planning and execution. HumanPen's project page describes the rule as "Plan first, start when you confirm", with five stages — Describe, Plan, In progress, In review, Delivered — and an API field, confirm_plan, that carries a spec_version so the plan you approved is the plan that ran.

2. How much can you upload — in total, and per file?

Why ask. Vendors quote three different units, and only one tells you whether your dataset fits. A page count, a per-file megabyte cap and a total intake allowance are not interchangeable.

How to check. Read the number and its unit. Humata's free tier is described as up to 60 pages a month. AskYourPDF's free plan is described as up to 100 pages per document with a 15MB max upload. Unriddle's free tier is described as 20MB or 120 pages per upload. Zotero's storage page lists 300 MB free — that is a reference library, not an analysis intake. HumanPen states Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip up to 5 GB total.

3. Will you get code you can actually run?

Why ask. A result you cannot rerun is a screenshot. The question is not whether the tool "supports Python" but whether a runnable script comes out the other end.

How to check. Search the deliverables section for the word "code" and check which language. HumanPen's page states analysis runs on Python or R, that work originally done in SPSS or Stata is redone in Python or R, and that code is delivered beside the .docx report.

4. Are the figures and tables submission-grade?

Why ask. A chart pasted from a chat window at screen resolution will be rejected by a production editor, and reformatting costs a day you did not budget.

How to check. Look for a stated resolution and a table convention. HumanPen's page states figures at 300 dpi to journal standards, three-line tables, and figure captions.

5. Is every reference checked one by one?

Why ask. Reference lists fail quietly: a DOI resolves to a different paper, a year is off by one. "Citations included" is a weaker promise than "citations verified".

How to check. Look for a verification count, not a citation-style count. HumanPen's review list states "All 11 references found, one by one", after a PubMed search with each DOI checked — the IST and CAST papers, Lancet 1997, in the published example.

6. How long do your files stay?

Why ask. Retention is the difference between a workspace and an archive. If the answer is measured in days, your analysis is a session, not an asset.

How to check. Julius's pricing page states 7-day file storage on Free, Plus, Pro and Business, and permanent file storage on Enterprise. HumanPen states projects are kept for 6 months after the last activity, with a reminder 3 days before expiry.

7. Is your material used for training?

Why ask. Unpublished data and a draft manuscript are the two things you least want in someone's training corpus.

How to check. Look for an explicit negative statement, not a privacy policy summary. HumanPen's page states material is not used for training, that each project runs in an isolated sandbox, and that projects are not shared across users. Where a page is silent, ask in writing before you upload.

8. Are failed runs billed?

Why ask. Long analyses fail — a malformed column, a timeout, a package that will not install. Whether that counts against you changes how freely you iterate.

How to check. Ask for a line about failures, not just a line about usage. HumanPen states that failed tasks are not billed. If the page you are reading does not address it, treat the question as open.

9. What is the unit of billing?

Why ask. Seats, pages, credits, words and usage cannot be compared without conversion, and the conversion depends on your project shape.

How to check. Identify the meter and what resets it. Humata meters pages, Julius meters credits, Unriddle prices per seat per month, Overleaf prices per user with 10 collaborators per project, Wordvice prices per word. HumanPen bills by actual usage in real time, states that a plan carries no price up front, and gives no figure until you confirm the plan — several dozen credits is the normal scoping range.

10. Can it be driven by an API?

Why ask. If the analysis runs again next quarter on new data, a UI-only tool means redoing the clicking.

How to check. Ask for the doc surface, not just the phrase "API". HumanPen publishes a seven-step flow — create a project and declare files, PUT to the upload target, call uploads/complete, send the first message, long-poll events, read state/stage/allowed_actions, answer input_required by id — plus signed webhooks containing project and state, an SSE stream at events/stream, a uniform code/message/data envelope, and an X-Request-ID on every response.

11. Can you request a revision without losing the old version?

Why ask. Reviewers ask for one more sensitivity analysis. If the revision overwrites the delivery, you can no longer show what changed.

How to check. Look for a two-part answer: scope confirmation and version retention. HumanPen states that a revision starts with the scope stated and confirmed, and that delivery produces a new version while keeping the old one.

12. Can you delete it?

Why ask. Ethics approvals and data-sharing agreements usually require a deletion route with a date.

How to check. Look for the word "delete" near the retention paragraph. HumanPen states projects can be deleted at any time.

Three common misconceptions

"The biggest upload number wins." Usually not, because the numbers are in different units. A 5 GB total intake, a 120-page per-upload cap and a 300 MB library allowance describe three different products. Convert to your own file first.

"A free tier tells you the ceiling." It tells you the entry point. Humata's free 60 pages a month, AskYourPDF's 100 pages per document and Unriddle's 20MB per upload are entry points; the ceiling is set by the paid tier and by the retention rule behind it.

"An API mention means my pipeline can call it." An API mention means a docs page exists. Check the auth model, whether events are polled or pushed, whether webhooks are signed, and whether every response carries an id you can trace in a log. Those four details decide whether the integration survives its second month.

FAQ

Is Zotero storage free? Yes — Zotero's storage page lists 300 MB free, with paid tiers above that. It is a reference library allowance, so it does not answer the question of how much raw data an analysis tool will accept.

Can Julius keep files longer than 7 days? Its pricing page states 7-day file storage on Free, Plus, Pro and Business, and permanent file storage on Enterprise. Longer retention is described only at that top tier.

Is a page limit the same as a token limit? No. A page limit is an intake rule about how much document text a tool will accept; a token limit is a model constraint. A tool can be generous on one and tight on the other, which is why the unit matters more than the number.

Do I need all twelve answered before I start? No. Questions 1, 2, 6, 7 and 12 are the ones to settle before you upload anything identifiable. The rest can be settled before you accept a delivery, and they are cheaper to negotiate then.
