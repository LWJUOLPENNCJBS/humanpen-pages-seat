Unriddle vs HumanPen Start Project: Buying Upload Volume vs Buying a Finished Report

Unriddle is the better choice when the work is reading and the constraint is how much you can put in front of the model at once. Its plans are built around upload size rather than output: Free allows 20 MB or 120 pages per upload, Plus ($10, or $8 per seat per month billed yearly) allows 100 MB or 600 pages, Pro ($20, or $16 billed yearly) allows 300 MB or 10,000 pages, and the Max tiers — Max 5x at $100 and Max 20x at $200 per seat per month, $80 and $160 billed yearly — remove the size and page limits on uploads.

HumanPen Start Project is the better choice when the work is producing and the constraint is what comes back. You attach data, get a plan, confirm it, and receive a .docx report, 300 dpi figures, three-line tables, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the Python or R code that produced every number, with a second AI reviewing the result before delivery.

One sells you a bigger window onto documents. The other sells you a document.

Two notes first. The pages we examined are branded Anara, not Unriddle: the pricing page header reads "Anara | Pricing" and both pages are footed "© 2026 Anara Labs, Inc". The pages we captured do not explain the relationship between the two names beyond sharing them, so if you arrive from a link about Unriddle, expect to land on Anara. Second, pricing is per seat and metered by usage, with credits on top.

How the workflows differ

1. A bigger window vs a finished artefact

Unriddle's ladder is about how much you can load. The free tier stops at 20 MB or 120 pages per upload; Plus takes you to 100 MB or 600 pages; Pro to 300 MB or 10,000 pages; Max removes the limit. Each step buys input capacity, which tells you what the product thinks is scarce: the size of the corpus you can hold in one conversation.

That is a useful thing to sell. A systematic review, a thesis with several hundred references, a regulatory dossier — these tasks are painful because of volume, and a tool that lets you put the whole set in one place and ask across it removes a real bottleneck.

HumanPen Start Project sells a different scarce thing. Intake is a flat 5 GB total per project across Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip — no tiers, no per-page arithmetic — and the differentiator is the output: a report written to journal conventions, figures at 300 dpi, three-line tables, speaker notes, and the code. The worked example on the page is a secondary analysis of the International Stroke Trial: 19,435 patients in the full analysis set, 62.2% of the aspirin group and 63.5% of the control group dead or dependent at 6 months, OR 0.95 (0.89–1.00), P = 0.072, with about 150 missing outcomes handled in a sensitivity analysis.

Reading 10,000 pages and computing a new number from a dataset are different jobs. The first is retrieval over text that already contains the answer; the second is estimation, and no amount of reading produces it.

2. A resetting meter vs a confirmed plan

Unriddle meters usage and shows it: a meter reports how much you have used and when it resets. On Free it refreshes daily; on paid plans it resets in full 7 days after you first use it. When the allowance runs out, work in progress finishes, new requests wait, and you can add credits or upgrade. Credits are bought in amounts from $5 to $1,000, and credits bought on the web expire 12 months after purchase.

That suits reading, which arrives in bursts. What to watch is the interaction between a weekly reset and a monthly budget: heavy and quiet weeks do not average out the way a monthly allowance would.

HumanPen's control is a checkpoint instead of a meter. The stages are Describe, Plan, In progress, In review, Delivered, and the rule on the page is "Plan first, start when you confirm". The project restates the brief — analysis set, endpoint, how missing outcomes are handled — and waits for approval before running anything. Revisions work the same way: you raise them in the project, the scope is explained first, work starts after you confirm, and old versions are kept.

For a reading session, a meter shows the cost of the next question. For an analysis, a plan is the better instrument: the expensive mistake is not the tenth question, it is the specification you never wrote down.

Uploading files and what comes back

Unriddle's intake is defined by the tier: 20 MB or 120 pages per upload on Free, 100 MB or 600 pages on Plus, 300 MB or 10,000 pages on Pro, and no size or page limit on the Max tiers. What comes back is answers drawn from the documents you uploaded; the document stays the unit of truth.

HumanPen Start Project defines intake as a total: 5 GB per project, any mix of Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip. Output is a package rather than a transcript — a .docx report, charts at 300 dpi to journal standards, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code. The page is explicit about limits too: analysis runs on Python or R, SPSS and Stata analyses are redone in Python or R, Wind and CNKI data must be exported first, and GPU training is not yet supported.

The difference in kind shows with a mixed folder: ten PDFs plus a survey export plus a Word template is one 5 GB-budget project on one side, and a set of uploads to be read on the other.

Price shape and governance

Unriddle prices per seat with volume tiers: Free at $0 with 20 MB or 120 pages per upload; Plus at $10 per seat per month, or $8 billed yearly, with 100 MB or 600 pages; Pro at $20, or $16 billed yearly, with 300 MB or 10,000 pages; Max 5x at $100 and Max 20x at $200 per seat per month, $80 and $160 billed yearly, with no size or page limit on uploads. Usage is metered on top of that, and credits from $5 to $1,000 extend it, with web-purchased credits expiring after 12 months.

HumanPen sells credits rather than subscription seats, so this comparison quotes no per-month figure for it. What is published is the commitment shape: a plan approved before work starts, scope explained before a revision begins, old versions retained, and projects kept for 6 months after the last activity with a reminder 3 days before expiry.

On governance the two pages address different readers. Unriddle's is written for a research team lead — seats, credits, upload limits. HumanPen's is written for someone answering a data-management question: transport encryption, an isolated sandbox per project, no sharing across users, no use for training, deletion at any time. The developer page adds an API at https://api.humanpen.net/v1, plan confirmation carrying a spec_version, short-lived signed URLs for the delivery zip, and webhooks that contain only the project and its state.

When to choose which

Choose Unriddle if the bottleneck is reading: you have a large corpus, the answers already exist in the text, and you need to ask across all of it. The free tier tests the behaviour on one real paper; Pro covers most literature reviews without a conversation about storage.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if the bottleneck is producing: the data needs a model run on it, the tables need to be built to a journal's conventions, and someone will ask how a figure was produced. The plan checkpoint, the second-AI review, the delivered code and the 6-month retention are all aimed at that question.

Use both if your paper has both bottlenecks, which most empirical papers do. Read and screen in Unriddle to decide what the literature says, then hand the dataset and the specification to HumanPen Start Project, confirm the plan, and take delivery of the report, figures and code. The reading tool settles what has been claimed; the project tool settles what your data shows.

The mismatch to avoid is asking a reading tool to recompute a result, or a project tool to summarise a corpus you have not triaged.
