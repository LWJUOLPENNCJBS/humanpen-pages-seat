A PhD student's first paper in one term: a week-by-week plan that survives HumanPen Start Project's plan step

The first paper is not hard because the analysis is hard. It is hard because six different jobs — cleaning, analysis, figures, writing, checking, formatting — are all invisible until one of them is late, and by then the deadline is fixed. A term is enough time if the jobs are sequenced, and nowhere near enough if they are attempted in parallel.

This is the sequence we would give a doctoral student who has data, a supervisor with limited time, and one term to produce a submittable manuscript with HumanPen Start Project doing the analysis and production work.

One to two: frame the question before touching the data

Write the question as a sentence with a comparison in it, then write the sentence you want to appear in the abstract. If the two sentences do not match, the study is not ready for analysis. This hour of writing prevents the most common first-paper failure, which is a technically competent analysis of a question nobody asked.

Bring that paragraph to your supervisor as a paragraph, not as a question. Supervisors are good at correcting a written claim and bad at inventing one from a conversation.

Two to three: make one dataset canonical

Decide which file is the source of truth and name it accordingly. Then produce the codebook: every variable, its unit, its coding, and its missing-value convention. Where a variable was derived, write the derivation.

HumanPen Start Project accepts Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip files up to 5 GB total, so you can hand over the raw exports, the codebook document and any prior reports in one upload rather than pre-merging them yourself. Pre-merging is where undocumented decisions enter the pipeline.

Three to four: treat the plan as the analysis contract

HumanPen Start Project confirms requirements, proposes a plan, and starts only once you approve it. That step exists because a misread requirement is cheap to fix in a plan and expensive to fix in a report. Read the plan with three questions: is the population the one I defined, is the endpoint at the timepoint I defined, and is the deliverable what my target journal needs.

The public example shows the level of specificity to expect: a full analysis set of 19,435 patients, a primary endpoint of death or dependency at six months, and about 150 missing outcomes handled in a sensitivity analysis. If a plan you receive is vaguer than that about your own study, ask for it to be tightened before you approve.

Five to six: inspect the run, do not just accept it

When the analysis comes back, spend an hour on inspection rather than on gratitude. Check that the population counts add up, that the endpoint in the tables is the endpoint in your protocol, and that a rerun reproduces the numbers. HumanPen Start Project reports these as explicit review items — every analysis population adds up, a clean rerun reproduces the results exactly — along with the primary endpoint matching (62.2% against 63.5%, P = 0.072 in the published example).

The figures arrive at 300 dpi with three-line tables and captions, which means your remaining job is editorial: decide which figure carries the argument and which belongs in a supplement.

Seven to eight: write results with the interpretation switched off

Write the results section with one rule: every sentence must be traceable to a table, a figure, or a stated method. No adjectives about importance, no explanation of mechanism, no comparison to the literature. This is not stylistic austerity; it is what makes the section reviewable.

Keep numbers exactly as computed. HumanPen Start Project's writing pass is designed to preserve numbers and citations while removing stock phrasing, and its public example shows a before-and-after on the same sentence with 62.2%, 63.5% and P = 0.072 unchanged. If a rewording changes a number, reject the rewording.

Nine to ten: write the framing last, and bound the claims

The introduction argues why the question matters; the discussion argues what the answer means and what it does not. Both are easier to write once the numbers are fixed, because you no longer have to hedge against an unknown result.

In the discussion, match verbs to evidence. A null result is "no evidence of a difference at six months", not "no effect". A subgroup pattern is a hypothesis, not a finding. Reviewers object to overreach far more often than to modest results.

Eleven: run an internal review that an outsider could repeat

Give the manuscript and the deliverables to someone who did not do the work, and ask them to check four things: the population arithmetic, the endpoint definition, the reproducibility of the numbers, and whether each reference exists and supports its sentence. The published example's fourth review item is that all 11 references were found one by one, which is the mechanical floor; the substantive check is whether each citation is apt.

Twelve: package for the journal, not for your desktop

Before submitting, confirm the figure resolution, the table style, the reference style, the word or page limit, the blinding requirements and the data availability statement. These are inexpensive to fix now and expensive to fix after a desk rejection.

If revisions are needed, HumanPen Start Project's flow explains the scope first, starts after you confirm, delivers a new version and keeps the old ones — which is useful when a co-author wants to see what changed between the submitted version and the revision.

What to do when the term goes wrong

Two failure modes account for most missed first-paper deadlines. The first is discovering in week ten that the endpoint was defined differently in the protocol and in the analysis; the plan step and the endpoint check exist to catch this early. The second is treating writing as the last two weeks rather than a parallel activity; start the methods section in week five, while the analysis is still fresh, and the final month becomes editing rather than authorship.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to know R or Python to use this?

No, but you should be able to read the code that was delivered. The analysis runs in Python or R, work originally done in SPSS or Stata is re-done in those languages, and the code ships with the report so your supervisor or a committee member can inspect it.

Can my supervisor see the plan before work starts?

Yes, and this is the practical reason to use the plan step. Export the plan, send it to your supervisor, and collect comments before you approve. Approval is a single decision point, so it is worth making it a shared one.

What if my data are messy?

Messy data are the normal case. The useful preparation is not cleaning everything, but documenting what you know: which rows are suspect, which values are impossible, which variables changed definition mid-study. Write that down in the brief and let the pipeline handle the cleaning with a record of what it did.

How long are the deliverables available?

Projects are kept for six months after the last activity, with a reminder three days before expiry, and can be deleted at any time. Download the report, figures and code when they are delivered; treat the project as a workspace rather than as an archive.
