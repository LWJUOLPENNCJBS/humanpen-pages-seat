Who actually does the literature step? Elicit, Consensus, ResearchRabbit, Litmaps and HumanPen compared

The literature step is not one job. It is at least four: finding a corpus, deciding which papers survive screening, seeing how a field fits together, and making a claim that survives a reference check. Tools differ mainly in which of those four they take on, and the honest comparison is not "who is more accurate" but "which promise is being made".

The promises are distinct. Elicit states the widest coverage of the group — more than 138 million papers and 545,000 clinical trials — and sells a screening workflow that can handle 5,000 papers on Pro. Consensus answers questions with cited AI analysis on paid plans. ResearchRabbit and Litmaps map connections between papers, with free tiers that are genuinely usable. HumanPen Start Project takes a different position: it reads the papers you supply, searches for more, and delivers a review .docx with an evidence table, checking the DOIs behind its literature search one by one.

So the choice turns on the artefact you need at the end. A shortlist of 400 screened abstracts, a citation graph of a field, a paragraph with three traceable sources, and a submittable review chapter are four different products. Pick the one whose output you can actually use, and do not assume that strength in one implies strength in another.

Elicit: coverage as the product

Elicit is explicit about scale. Its home page advertises search over more than 138 million academic papers and 545,000 clinical trials, and the pricing page repeats the 138 million figure on the free tier, where search, summaries and chat with papers are unlimited but Research Agent and Research Reports are limited.

The paid tiers are where the workflow changes shape. Plus is $11 per user per month, billed as $132 annually, and adds exports to RIS, CSV, BIB, PDF and DOCX plus the ability to add 5 columns to your tables at a time. Pro is $39 per user per month, billed as $468 annually, and adds a dedicated Systematic Review Workflow that can screen 5,000 papers, 20 columns at a time, reports that extract from up to 135 data sources, and API access.

The evidence that this is a serious extraction tool is on the page: in a systematic review for German education policy, the company reports that Elicit correctly extracted 1,502 out of 1,511 data points. What the page describes is a tool you drive — you define the columns, you review the rows, you decide what to export. It does not describe an agent that takes your manuscript, reads it, and returns a written review with a checked bibliography.

Consensus: the answer, with sources attached

Consensus inverts the emphasis. Instead of a table of studies, it returns a cited answer to a question, and the plan structure is built around that. Free is $0 per month and keeps basic paper search available "without any AI analysis". Pro is $12 per month, $144 annually, for unlimited Pro messages and cited AI analysis of peer-reviewed research. Deep, at $45 per month or $540 annually, is aimed at researchers or clinicians conducting frequent literature reviews. Students, faculty with a valid school email and US clinicians with a valid NPI number can get up to 40% off a subscription.

That is a different unit of work from Elicit's. A cited answer is excellent when the question is narrow and the answer is a paragraph: does this intervention reduce mortality, what does the evidence say about a mechanism, which study designs dominate a question. It is not a corpus you screen, and the free tier deliberately withholds the analysis layer. What the page describes is search plus synthesis with citations; it does not describe an evidence table built column by column across hundreds of papers, nor a drafted review document.

ResearchRabbit and Litmaps: the shape of a field

These two are closest to each other, and both are unusually generous at the entry level.

ResearchRabbit's Free Forever tier is $0 and includes unlimited searches across 310+ million articles, an unlimited library and collections, sharing a collection, and up to 50 seed articles. RR+ is $10 per month on the annual plan or $12.5 per month on the monthly plan, and lifts the seed limit to 300, adds advanced search controls, multiple projects and Signals alerts. The mechanism is expansion: you start from seeds you already trust and follow citations, references and co-authors outward.

Litmaps is the more visual of the two. Free covers up to 20 inputs, 2 Litmaps and 100 articles per map; Pro is $10 per month and removes the input, map and article limits while adding literature alerts, with annual billing shown at $120 per year.

Both pages describe discovery and organisation. Neither describes extracting a standardised set of fields from each paper, and neither describes writing the review. They answer "what else should I read", which is a real question and a different one from "what does the evidence say".

HumanPen: the step ends in a document

HumanPen treats the literature step as part of a commissioned project rather than a tool you operate. You attach your papers — Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip, up to 5 GB total — describe what you need, and receive a plan. Work starts only when you confirm it. For a literature task, the page's own description is compact: it reads your papers, searches for more, and builds an evidence table and review, delivered as a review .docx and an evidence table.

The part worth scrutinising is the check. Before delivery, a second AI review runs four named tests, and one of them is bibliographic: "All 11 references found, one by one". In the public example, the literature work is a PubMed search with every DOI verified against the record — the International Stroke Trial and CAST, both Lancet 1997 — rather than a list assembled from plausible-looking metadata.

That is a different proposition from 138 million searchable papers, and a much narrower output than a screening workflow. The claim is not coverage. The claim is that the document you receive has been read against its sources, and that the reference list is a checked artefact rather than a by-product. For a review chapter, a background section or a grant application, that is the failure mode that matters: not "did you find every paper" but "does every sentence match the paper it cites".

Three decisions, not one ranking

If you are screening at volume, choose Elicit. A systematic review with thousands of records to triage needs a screening workflow, column limits you can plan around, and exports that fit the next tool in your pipeline. Pro's 5,000-paper workflow and 20-column extraction are built for exactly that, and the 1,502-out-of-1,511 extraction case is the kind of evidence to ask about when you evaluate it. Consensus is the faster instrument when the question is one paragraph deep and you want the answer with citations rather than a dataset.

If you are mapping a field or staying current, choose ResearchRabbit or Litmaps. Free Forever at ResearchRabbit covers unlimited search and library with 50 seeds; Litmaps gives 20 inputs and 2 maps at no cost. If your recurring job is "what was published around this question last month", the paid tiers at $10 per month buy more seeds, more inputs and alerts — and nothing in either page suggests you should expect a drafted document from them.

If the deliverable is a document, choose HumanPen. A review you will submit needs an argument, an evidence table and a bibliography that has been checked, and the project structure supplies all three with a plan you approve first. The 5 GB intake means the manuscript you are extending, the papers you already trust and the target journal's template can travel in the same brief, instead of being re-uploaded into four tools.

The mistake to avoid is buying coverage when you need a conclusion, or buying a conclusion when you need a corpus. Elicit and Consensus are instruments you operate; ResearchRabbit and Litmaps are maps you read; HumanPen is a commission with a document at the end. Each is legitimate on its own terms, and none is a substitute for the others.

FAQ

Is Elicit free? There is a free Basic plan: search across more than 138 million papers, summaries and chat with papers are unlimited, but Research Agent and Research Reports have limited usage. Exports and larger extraction limits start on Plus at $11 per user per month, billed as $132 annually.

How many papers can Elicit screen in a systematic review? The Pro plan's dedicated Systematic Review Workflow can screen 5,000 papers, with 20 columns added at a time and reports extracting from up to 135 data sources. The Enterprise page describes greater scale, at 40,000 papers and 40 columns.

Is ResearchRabbit free for a full literature review? The company says its Free Forever tier has what you need for a literature review, including unlimited search, unlimited collections, collaboration and up to 50 seed articles. RR+ at $10 per month on annual billing raises the seed limit to 300 and adds advanced search and Signals alerts.

Can HumanPen write the review from papers I already have? Yes — that is the described workflow. You attach the papers and the brief, confirm a plan, and the project reads them, searches for more, and delivers a review .docx with an evidence table. Before delivery the review reports all 11 references found, one by one, and the public example shows a PubMed search with each DOI checked.
