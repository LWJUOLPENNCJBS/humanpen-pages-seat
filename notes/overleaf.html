Overleaf vs HumanPen Start Project: Setting the Paper, or Producing It

Overleaf is the right choice when the paper already exists as prose and the problem is the document: LaTeX, the template, the compile, the co-author who wants to see track changes on a Sunday. The free tier is listed at $0, the individual paid plan is listed at $16.75 with $199/year and $25 alongside it on the same page, that plan allows 10 collaborators per project, and there are thousands of free templates to start from.

HumanPen Start Project is the right choice when the paper does not yet have numbers. You describe the study and attach the data; a plan comes back and work starts when you confirm it. The analysis runs in Python or R, a second AI reviews it before delivery, and what arrives is a .docx report, 300 dpi figures, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes and the code.

These two are not rivals. One is where the text is set, the other where the text comes from. A group can and often should use both: the project produces results, figures and a draft, and Overleaf turns them into a submission-ready PDF in the journal's class file. The question is which half of the job you are short of.

Two different bottlenecks

Most papers stall in one of two places, and the two products address different ones.

The first stall is the document. The analysis is done, the figures sit as PNGs in a folder, and someone has to fight a two-column class file, match the journal's reference style, keep tables from breaking across pages and reconcile four authors' comments. Overleaf is built for this: online LaTeX editing with collaboration at its centre, and thousands of free templates, including journal formats, so the class file is a starting point rather than an afternoon's work.

The second stall is upstream of that. There is a dataset, a question and a deadline, and nothing in between. No table one, no model, no figure, no methods paragraph that says what was actually done. That is the gap HumanPen Start Project is aimed at, and the workflow is explicit about it: five stages — Describe, Plan, In progress, In review, Delivered — under the rule "Plan first, start when you confirm".

Stuck at the first stall, a typesetting tool is the correct purchase. Stuck at the second, no amount of template quality will produce a results section.

What "collaboration" means in each

Overleaf's collaboration is synchronous and document-shaped. The paid individual plan allows 10 collaborators per project, and the page's FAQ explains the arrangement: only the subscriber's account is upgraded, and invited collaborators get the paid features on the projects they share with the subscriber. For a paper with a statistician, a senior author and a student, that is the right unit — a shared document with a shared history.

HumanPen's collaboration is asynchronous and specification-shaped. There is no shared cursor: you write a brief, you get a plan, you confirm it or push back, and the plan is versioned so a change of mind is a new version rather than an edit someone has to notice. The review is a second AI reading the output against the plan, and delivery is a package rather than a live document.

The two forms stack without conflict. Confirming a plan does not require co-authors in the same file, and a shared LaTeX document does not require the numbers to have been produced in it.

Where the numbers and the figures come from

This is the substantive difference, and it is worth being concrete about the handover.

The public HumanPen example is a secondary analysis of the International Stroke Trial. The plan restates the brief as a full analysis set of 19,435 patients, a primary endpoint of death or dependency at 6 months, and about 150 missing outcomes handled with a sensitivity analysis. Delivery includes the endpoint at 62.2% in the aspirin group against 63.5% in controls, an odds ratio of 0.95 (0.89–1.00) and P = 0.072.

Those are the numbers a results section is made of, and they arrive with the material a typesetter needs: three-line tables with captions, figures at 300 dpi to journal standard, and the code that produced them. The review that precedes delivery checks the endpoint against the analysis, checks that every analysis population adds up, reruns the analysis cleanly to confirm the numbers reproduce, and reports "all 11 references found, one by one" — plus, in the example, a PubMed search with each DOI checked individually.

Overleaf's contribution starts one step later, and it is not a small one. Getting a 300 dpi TIFF and a three-line table into a journal's two-column layout, with the caption style the journal demands and the reference list in its house format, is real work that a .docx does not do for you. The pages we examined describe templates and collaborative editing; they do not describe running the analysis that fills the table.

The division of labour: the project owns the claim, the figure and the number; Overleaf owns the artefact the journal's system will accept.

Uploads, formats and what lands on your desk

Overleaf takes text: you write LaTeX or use the editor's aids, compile, and get a PDF. The intake is the source and the output is the compiled document.

HumanPen takes a project. The intake is stated precisely: Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip, up to 5 GB total — sized for a study's messy reality (dataset, codebook, protocol, reviewer's comments, previous draft) rather than for one manuscript file.

The outputs are correspondingly different in kind. Overleaf returns a PDF. The project returns a .docx report, charts at 300 dpi, three-line tables and captions, a .pptx with speaker notes for the lab meeting, and the code. It also handles the writing step with a constraint worth noting: a de-AI-tone pass that leaves numbers and citations exactly as they are, with a public before-and-after example that keeps the same percentages and the same P value, rephrased into a shorter sentence rather than rounded.

The consequence: a .docx is not a submission file, and a PDF is not a draft you can revise. For a LaTeX journal the .docx is an input to Overleaf, not a replacement; for a report or a thesis chapter it may be the end of the line.

Price and governance

Overleaf's pricing is published and simple. The free tier is $0. The individual paid plan appears at $16.75, and the same block also shows $199/year and $25; we report the page as it stands rather than converting between them, since the page sets them side by side. A two-user option exists on the same table.

HumanPen does not publish a per-seat rate, because it does not sell seats. Billing is metered by actual usage, failed tasks are not billed, and the plan carries no price up front — scoping typically costs tens of credits, and the figure becomes concrete once there is a plan to cost. A round limit stops the project and asks before continuing; a per-project credit ceiling caps the exposure. When billing.settled is true, the number is final.

Governance differs in kind, not degree. Overleaf's model is a hosted editor: the source sits in an account, and the protections are the account's. HumanPen's page commits to encryption in transit, an isolated sandbox per project, no sharing across users and no use for training, plus deletion at any time and retention of six months after the last activity with a reminder three days before expiry.

For most manuscripts the first is adequate: a draft is not usually the sensitive asset. For a dataset with identifiable patients, the second set is the one to read before uploading, and the per-project sandbox is the part that makes it defensible to a governance committee.

When to choose which

Choose Overleaf if the content exists and the document is the problem. A free tier at $0 is enough to test the workflow, 10 collaborators per project covers a normal author list, and thousands of free templates mean the class file is usually already there. For settled results, a typesetting tool is the shortest path to submission.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if the results are not settled, or the writing is the bottleneck rather than the layout. A dataset that needs a table one, a model, 300 dpi figures and a methods paragraph is a delivery problem. The plan-first structure shows you population, endpoint and missing-data handling before compute is spent, and the review checks the numbers in the text against the numbers in the analysis.

Use both if you are writing for a journal that wants LaTeX. Commission the analysis and take delivery of the .docx, the 300 dpi figures, the tables and the code; then move the text into Overleaf, drop the figures in at the delivered resolution and set the class file. The project's job ends at a defensible claim, Overleaf's at a PDF the submission system accepts.

The failure mode is commissioning analysis and typesetting as one undifferentiated task. Their acceptance criteria differ: a results section is accepted when a reviewer believes it, a PDF when the journal's build succeeds.

FAQ

Is Overleaf free? There is a free tier listed at $0. The individual paid plan is listed at $16.75, with $199/year and $25 shown on the same page; we report those as the page presents them rather than converting between billing periods.

How many people can collaborate on an Overleaf project? The individual paid plan allows 10 collaborators per project, and the page's FAQ notes that only the subscriber's account is upgraded — collaborators get the paid features on the shared project. A two-user option is also listed.

Can Overleaf run my statistical analysis? The pages we examined describe online LaTeX editing, collaboration and templates. They do not describe executing a statistical analysis on your data, so we make no claim either way; the analysis in a HumanPen project runs in Python or R, in an isolated sandbox, with the code delivered alongside the report.

Can I use Overleaf and HumanPen for the same paper? Yes, and for a LaTeX journal it is the natural arrangement. The project delivers a .docx report, 300 dpi figures, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes and the code; Overleaf takes the text and figures into the journal's class file. The intake for the project is Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip up to 5 GB total.
