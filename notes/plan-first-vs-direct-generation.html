Plan first or generate first? How HumanPen and three workspace tools fix the scope

Every analysis tool answers one question, whether or not it says so out loud: at what moment does the job stop moving? In a workspace tool the answer is "when you ask for something and it runs". In HumanPen Start Project the answer is written down — the request comes back as a plan, and the work waits for you to confirm it.

That difference decides who owns the scope. If the scope lives in a prompt, a notebook cell or an AI task, the person at the keyboard owns it, and it changes each time the keyboard is used. If it lives in a confirmed plan, both sides can point at the same document a month later, when a co-author asks why a table has a particular denominator.

So the choice is not about capability. Pick a workspace when the work is exploratory and you want the answer inside the session. Pick HumanPen when the work ends in a file someone else will hold you to, and agreeing the scope in advance is worth more than starting immediately.

Three entry points, three ideas of "starting"

Each tool opens a job differently, and that is where the scope question is settled.

Julius opens with a workspace. The pricing page describes one place covering analysis and research, presentations, reports, websites, images and video, priced in credits: daily credits on Free, 2,000 a month on Plus, 5,000 on Pro, 60,000 on Business. That suits someone with a question and no fixed idea of the artefact. The page describes the entry point as a request inside the workspace; it does not describe a scoping document signed off before the first computation.

Deepnote opens with a notebook. The page describes collaborative notebooks in the cloud, scheduling, code reviews, comment and version history, and it prices compute rather than output: unlimited Basic machines with 5 GB RAM and 2 vCPU on Free, Team at $39 per editor/month billed yearly with the annual figure marked −20%, and machine examples to 4 vCPU / 8 GB. A notebook is a good container for the scope — visible in the cells, and changing shape as you type.

Rows opens with a spreadsheet and an AI task. The page frames it as an AI data analyst that extracts from PDFs, builds live reports and lets you "tell AI what to do, and let it do the work", priced in AI Tasks — 5 a month on Free, 200 on Plus at $8 per user/month, 1,000 on Pro at $79 per month plus $8 per user — with file import size rising 1 MB, 5 MB, 100 MB. The unit of scope and the unit of cost are the same task, which is unusually legible.

HumanPen opens with a description. You say what the project is and attach the files — Excel, CSV, Word, PDF or zip, up to 5 GB total. What comes back is not an answer but a plan, and the rule on the page is Plan first, start when you confirm: five stages, Describe, Plan, In progress, In review, Delivered.

When the scope stops moving

There are two honest ways to run a project and they fail differently.

When the scope is set at the moment of execution, the failure mode is drift: the third question differs subtly from the first, the figure is rebuilt for a different subset, and nobody can say which version produced the table in the draft. Its virtue is speed, and it is the right model for exploration — a notebook that grows over an afternoon is not a governance problem, it is how people think.

When the scope is fixed before execution, the failure mode is delay: you decide what you want before you see anything, and deciding is work. The virtue is that after confirmation the definition cannot quietly change — and if it must, the page describes a revision path where the new scope is stated first, confirmed, and delivered as a new version with the old one kept.

The practical difference shows up in the review stage. The workspace pages describe review as collaboration — Deepnote lists code reviews, comment and version history, and Rows offers real-time collaboration and feedback from your team — but the review is of what was produced. HumanPen's review is also of what was produced, run by a second AI before delivery against four named checks: the primary endpoint matches, every analysis population adds up, a clean rerun reproduces the results exactly, and all 11 references are found one by one. In the public example, the endpoint check reads 62.2% versus 63.5%, P = 0.072.

A plan plus a second reader is a different kind of artefact from a session: both can be audited.

Can you see the cost before the scope locks?

In a credit or task model you can see the meter before you commit. Julius lists the monthly credit allowance per tier, Rows lists the AI Task count, and Deepnote prices the machine, so a heavy job's cost is legible in vCPU and RAM. If the question is "what will an afternoon of exploration cost me", those pages answer it before you start.

HumanPen inverts it. A plan carries no price up front: you pay for scoping — usually a few dozen credits — and the confirmed plan is what the run is measured against. The page says where the guardrails sit instead: a round stops and asks at its credit limit, a per-project ceiling caps the total, failed tasks are not charged, and figures are final once billing settles.

For a buyer who needs a number before anyone signs anything, the credit model is the easier conversation, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. For a buyer who needs the number not to move afterwards, plan-plus-ceiling is stronger: you agree a specification, then a meter that cannot run past a ceiling you set. The two are not exclusive — many groups scope a pilot in a metered tool, then commission the deliverable with a ceiling informed by it.

The five stages, and what each one hands over

In Describe you supply the task and the files. In Plan you receive the specification: analysis set, endpoint, sensitivity analyses, deliverables. In In progress the work runs in Python or R in an isolated sandbox, encrypted in transit, never shared across users. In In review the second AI runs the four checks above. In Delivered you get a .docx report, figures at 300 dpi to journal standards, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code.

Only the last stage produces the thing most people are paid to produce, which is the argument for the model: the earlier four exist so the fifth does not have to be redone.

Decision rules

Run it in a workspace if the question is still forming. A first look at a dataset, a set of candidate figures, a quick model to see whether an effect is worth pursuing — Julius, Deepnote and Rows are each built for that loop, and their metering makes its cost visible. Paying for a plan before you know what you are asking would be premature.

Commission a project if the endpoint is a document with a fixed audience: a chapter, a report, a submission, a re-analysis a co-author will check line by line. Here the plan does work a prompt cannot, because it fixes the denominators before the numbers exist.

Use both if the work has an exploratory front and a documentary back. Explore in the metered tool until the specification is stable, then hand that version to HumanPen and keep the notebook as the working record and the .docx plus code as the defensible one.

The mismatch to avoid is commissioning a deliverable before the question is settled — or exploring until nobody can reconstruct which version produced the submitted table.

FAQ

Does Julius require me to approve a plan before it starts work? The pricing page we examined describes the workspace, the credit allowances and the file-storage window; it does not describe a confirmation step before a request is handled. HumanPen's page describes one: the plan is returned first, and work begins on confirmation.

Is Deepnote a replacement for a statistical report? Its page describes collaborative notebooks, scheduled runs, code reviews and dashboards. It does not describe a .docx report, journal-standard figures or a slide deck with speaker notes, so we make no claim either way.

What happens if the scope changes after I confirm a HumanPen plan? The page describes a revision path: the new scope is stated, confirmed, and delivered as a new version while the old one is kept. In a workspace the scope changes as you type, and the earlier state survives only if the file was saved.

Can I see the price before confirming a plan? Not on the plan itself — a plan carries no price up front. You see the shape of the commitment: scoping usually costs a few dozen credits, a round stops at its limit and asks, and a per-project ceiling caps the total.
