A deck you can still edit: speaker notes, source figures and HumanPen

There are two kinds of slide deck, and they are judged by different people.

The first is a poster that happens to be 16:9: one strong image, three words, generated in a minute and forgotten by Thursday. The second is the deck you present at a group meeting, a progress review or a viva — where a supervisor will ask why panel B has a different denominator, where the figure has to be the figure from the paper, and where you need to know what to say when the slide is on the screen.

The second kind has three requirements that are easy to state and hard to satisfy together: the file must be editable, the speaker notes must exist, and the figures must come from the same analysis as the tables. QuillBot and Julius both describe presentation generation inside a broader tool. HumanPen delivers a .pptx with speaker notes, and the figures in it are the 300 dpi figures from the report.

What a room actually asks of a slide

Three questions get asked in every group meeting, and none of them is "is this slide attractive".

"What is that number?" The number on the slide has to be the number in the table, which has to be the number in the analysis. A deck built separately from the analysis is a second place for the same fact to live, and second places drift.

"Why is that bar grey?" Someone will ask about a detail you did not choose consciously, and the answer has to be in your notes or in your head. Speaker notes are the difference between presenting and improvising.

"Can you send me that slide?" The answer needs to be a file the recipient can open, edit and paste into their own deck. A rendered image, a link or a PDF ends the conversation; a .pptx continues it.

That is the whole specification. It is undramatic, and it is why decks for internal review are a different product category from decks for a launch.

Julius: presentations as one output of a workspace

Julius approaches the same step from the other direction. Its pricing page describes one AI workspace for analysis and creation — analyse data and research, create presentations, generate reports and dashboards, build websites, generate professional images, create and edit video — and presentations are listed as one of those outputs rather than as a separate product.

The advantage is continuity. If the analysis and the deck are produced in the same workspace, the chart on the slide can come from the same data the analysis used, without an export-and-reimport step in the middle. That is the same argument HumanPen makes, arriving from a different architecture.

How HumanPen produces the deck

HumanPen's delivery list is short and specific: a .docx report, figures at 300 dpi to journal standards, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code.

Read that list in order and the deck stops being a separate task. The analysis runs in Python or R in an isolated sandbox. The report and the figures come out of that run. The slides are built from the same material, which is why the figure on slide 4 can be the same figure as figure 2 in the manuscript rather than a screenshot of it.

Three properties follow.

The file is editable. A .pptx is a working format: your supervisor can delete a slide, your co-author can paste one into their deck, the conference template can be applied. This matters more than it sounds, because most of the work on an academic deck happens after the first version — trimming for time, reordering for the argument, cutting the panel that invites the wrong question.

The notes are part of the deliverable. Speaker notes are what turn a deck into a talk: what to say on the methods slide, where to skip, which number to emphasise. A deck without notes is a document you read aloud.

The figures carry their provenance. Because the figures are the 300 dpi journal figures, the deck inherits the checking that went into them: a second AI review before delivery that includes the primary endpoint matching, every analysis population adding up, and a clean rerun reproducing the results exactly. If a reviewer asks where the number on slide 7 came from, the answer is a table in the report and a line in the code.

The trade is the same as everywhere else in this workflow: you do not get a canvas. There is no drag-to-reposition; a change of structure is a revision, stated, confirmed and delivered as a new version with the old one kept. For one deck per project that is a reasonable exchange. For a weekly seminar rotation, it is not.

A deck that passes all four survives the two events that actually happen: a co-author edits it without you, and a reviewer asks where a number came from.

Decision rules

Generate, if the deck is disposable. A journal club summary, a five-minute lab update, a first draft to react to. A free presentation maker inside a writing suite, or slides from a workspace you are already paying for, gets you to something on screen faster than any process, and the cost of being wrong is fifteen minutes.

Build in your own tool, if the deck is a performance. A conference talk you will give six times, a lecture you will reuse, a viva where the sequencing is the argument. Here you want direct control over layout and timing, and you want the file on your own machine under your own version control.

Commission it with the document, if the deck must agree with the paper. A defence where the slide and the table must tell the same story, an internal review where the figures are the submission figures. The value is not the slide design; it is that the deck, the report and the code are one artefact set, reviewed together.

The failure to avoid is presenting a number that cannot be traced — a slide built from a screenshot of a table that was rebuilt after the slide was made.
