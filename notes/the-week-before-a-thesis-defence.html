The week before a thesis defence: turning finished work into slides with HumanPen Start Project

By the week before a defence, the analysis is done and the thesis is bound. What is missing is the thing the committee will actually experience: a talk that argues the thesis in forty minutes without misrepresenting a single number, plus answers to the questions you have been avoiding for three years.

This is the sequence we would use for that week, with HumanPen Start Project producing the slides from the finished work. The governing rule is simple: the slides are a compression of the thesis, not a second analysis.

Seven days out: write the argument, not the outline

One page, three sentences: the claim the thesis makes, the evidence that supports it, and why that is a contribution. If the three sentences take more than a page, the talk will take more than an hour.

Everything in the deck answers to this page. A slide that does not serve the claim is either an appendix slide or a deletion.

Six days out: choose the figures before designing slides

List the figures in the order the argument needs them, not in the order the thesis presents them. For each, write the sentence it is supposed to support. A figure with no sentence is decoration, and decoration is what makes a defence feel long.

If a figure needs redrawing for the talk — larger labels, fewer panels, a clearer reference line — do it now. HumanPen Start Project delivers figures at 300 dpi to journal standards, which is more resolution than a projector needs and exactly what you want if the committee asks for the file afterwards.

Five days out: one sentence per slide

Write the deck as a list of sentences first, one per slide, in order. This is faster to revise than slides and it exposes structural problems immediately: two sentences that make the same point, a gap between the methods and the results, a conclusion that arrives before its evidence.

Only then decide the visual form of each slide: a chart, a table, a schematic, a quotation. A deck built this way survives a committee member asking "what is this slide for", because the answer is the sentence.

Four days out: produce an editable deck with speaker notes

The deliverable to ask for is a .pptx that opens in PowerPoint or Keynote with the template intact, and speaker notes that contain what you intend to say rather than what is on the slide. HumanPen Start Project delivers .pptx slides with speaker notes as part of its project deliverables, and the slides are editable in and out, which matters when your supervisor wants to move a section the night before.

Give the brief the constraints you actually have: the department template, the time limit, the number of slides the committee expects, and the figures to use. A deck produced against stated constraints needs less rework than a deck produced against a topic.

Three days out: audit every number on every slide

Take the deck and the thesis side by side and check each number: same value, same rounding, same denominator, same units. Then check the endpoints and the labels. Committees ask about discrepancies between the talk and the document, and "the slide rounded differently" is an avoidable answer.

This is the same discipline the product applies to written deliverables — numbers kept exactly as computed, the primary endpoint in the example stated as 62.2% against 63.5% with P = 0.072 — and it is worth applying to the deck even when the deck was assembled by hand.

Two days out: rehearse with someone who will interrupt

Two full rehearsals. The first for timing, with a clock and no interruptions. The second with a listener instructed to interrupt with the questions you least want: why this population, why this model, what would change your mind, what is the alternative explanation.

Record the second one. Most people discover that their timing problem is in the first five minutes, and that their answer to the alternative-explanation question is three sentences too long.

One day out: prepare answers, not slides

Write the six questions you fear, with a three-sentence answer each: the direct answer, the evidence, and the limitation. Keep the notes with you. A prepared limitation reads as command of the field; an improvised one reads as a gap.

Do not add slides on the last day. A deck that grows on the eve of a defence is a deck nobody has rehearsed.

After the defence: the version that gets archived

Committees often ask for the deck, and examiners sometimes ask for the analysis. Download the report, figures, tables and code when they are delivered rather than relying on continued access; projects are kept for six months after the last activity with a reminder three days before expiry, and can be deleted at any time.

The handout, and the questions that come after

Some committees want a handout: the argument on one page, the key table, the key figure. If yours does, produce it from the deck rather than from the thesis, so the numbers match what you said aloud.

Prepare for the conversation after the talk, which is longer than the talk and less predictable. Three questions recur: what is the alternative explanation, what would falsify the claim, and what would you do next. Each deserves a three-sentence answer that names the limitation before the committee does, because a limitation you have already stated cannot be used against you.

Regenerate nothing else after this point. A deck that is edited on the morning of the defence is a deck whose timing nobody has tested, and the section you moved will be the one that runs long.

What to do if the committee interrupts early

Some committees stop you at the third slide and ask about the method. Answer the question, then ask whether they would like the rest of the talk or a discussion. Both are acceptable outcomes, and neither requires you to abandon the argument you prepared — the argument page from seven days out is the thing you can deliver in any order, which is exactly why it was written first.

Frequently asked questions

Can the deck be built from the thesis file directly?

It can be built from the thesis plus the delivered report and figures, which is the better input because the report already contains the tables and figures at their final values. Give the brief the argument page as well; without it, a deck summarises rather than argues.

What if my supervisor wants to rewrite the slides?

Ask for an editable .pptx, then edit. The speaker notes are the part worth preserving, because they encode the argument you rehearsed.

How many slides should a defence have?

Follow your department's expectation rather than a rule from the internet. When the constraint is a time limit, the useful calculation is sentences per minute: a talk of forty minutes holds roughly the number of claims you can say aloud in forty minutes, which is fewer than most first drafts assume.

Do I need to mention that slides were produced with assistance?

Follow your institution's rules on declaring assistance, and describe contributions accurately wherever they are declared. The substantive requirement is that you can defend every claim and every number in the deck.
