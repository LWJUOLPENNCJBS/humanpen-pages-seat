IBM SPSS Statistics vs HumanPen Start Project: Seats and Modules vs a Confirmed Plan

SPSS is the stronger choice when an institution needs a statistics package that everyone already knows. The public page sells it the way enterprises buy software: a Base subscription "Starting at $99.00 USD Per Authorized User", three add-on packages — custom tables and advanced statistics, forecasting and decision trees, complex sampling and testing — each "Starting at $79.00 USD Per Authorized User", a free trial, and academic routes that include campus-wide access for institutions and single-user student licences at special academic pricing. Add the 2026 TrustRadius Buyer's Choice Award in the Statistical Analysis category, and you have a product an IT committee can approve without a debate.

HumanPen Start Project is the stronger choice when one person needs one finished analysis. You describe the task and attach the data, the system returns a plan, and nothing runs until you confirm it. The analysis is carried out in Python or R, a second AI reviews it, and what comes back is a .docx report with 300 dpi figures, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code that produced every number.

The difference is in the unit of purchase. SPSS is bought per authorised user, per module, per year; the value accumulates across a department and depreciates if people stop using it. HumanPen is bought per project, by usage; the value lands in a document. That is why the two rarely compete for the same budget line, and why the useful question is not "which is better" but "who is going to sit in front of the software".

How the workflows differ

1. A seat is a capability; a plan is a commitment

Buying SPSS buys a capability that sits with a person: for as long as the subscription runs, that authorised user can run whatever the licensed modules allow. It is a good instrument for a department, because it is stable, forecastable and familiar — you know what the cost is per person per year, and you know what each user can do. The 2026 TrustRadius Buyer's Choice Award in the Statistical Analysis category is the kind of signal procurement teams look for, and it speaks to the product's standing rather than to any one analysis.

Buying a HumanPen project buys an output. The plan comes first, and it is specific: in the public worked example, a secondary analysis of the International Stroke Trial, it fixes the full analysis set at 19,435 patients, the primary endpoint as death or dependency at 6 months, and a sensitivity analysis for about 150 missing outcomes — then waits for a human to agree. You are not acquiring the ability to analyse; you are commissioning one analysis, with the scope written down.

Both are honest ways to spend a research budget. They differ in what happens when the work stops: a seat keeps its capability until the renewal, and a project ends when it is delivered.

2. The module ladder vs one execution path

SPSS's page is organised as Base plus three add-on packages, each priced separately at "Starting at $79.00 USD Per Authorized User": custom tables and advanced statistics, forecasting and decision trees, complex sampling and testing. The structure is legible — you buy the capabilities your work needs — and it also means a scoping conversation before every project: does this analysis need the forecasting package, or will Base do?

HumanPen has one execution path and states its edges: analysis runs in Python or R; analyses originally done in SPSS or Stata are performed using Python or R; Wind or CNKI data must be exported first; GPU training is not yet supported. That flatness is a limitation as much as a simplification. There is no module to add if your method needs one — the answer is either that the method can be expressed in Python or R, or that this is not the right tool.

For a lab that runs a fixed repertoire of tests, the module ladder is a precise fit. For a one-off analysis that ends in a report, specifying a path is more useful than assembling a stack.

3. What "reproducible" is supposed to mean at handover

The most useful question to ask of either product is what a colleague receives when the work is done.

HumanPen's answer is explicit. Before delivery there is a review with four checks stated as results: the primary endpoint matches (62.2% vs 63.5%, P = 0.072 in the example), every analysis population adds up, a clean rerun reproduces the results exactly, and all 11 references are found, one by one. What arrives is a .docx report, figures at 300 dpi to journal standards, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code — so the rerun is available to the recipient, not only to the analyst.

The SPSS page we examined describes the platform's analytical capabilities, the licensing options and the academic routes; it does not describe a delivered code artefact or a report package assembled for handover, so we do not claim either way. Practically, reproducibility in SPSS has always depended on the analyst saving syntax alongside output — a discipline, not a product feature, and one that varies by team.

Uploading data and what comes back

Our capture of the SPSS page does not state a data-size limit, an accepted-format list or a per-user storage allowance, so we assert none of them. What it does state is the commercial shape: a free trial, Base at "Starting at $99.00 USD Per Authorized User", three add-ons at "Starting at $79.00 USD Per Authorized User" each, campus-wide access for institutions, and student licences at special academic pricing through IBM-authorised vendors.

HumanPen states intake and limits together: Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip up to 5 GB total, analysis in Python or R, no GPU training yet. The delivery is the part that differs from a platform: a report, journal-standard figures, three-line tables with captions, a slide deck with speaker notes, and the code. If your output is a chapter, a submission or a committee paper, that is closer to the artefact than a results viewer is.

Price shape and governance

SPSS is priced per authorised user, with a Base subscription and three add-on packages at the levels quoted above, plus campus-wide and student routes for academic institutions. The arithmetic is easy to present to a committee: multiply by seats, add the modules each group needs, and you have a number for the year. The trade is that the number is incurred whether or not the work gets done, and that a heavy user and a light user cost the same.

HumanPen's arithmetic is the inverse. Work is billed in real time by what it consumes, failed tasks are not charged, and a plan carries no price up front — scoping a request is charged, usually a few dozen credits. A round stops and asks once it has used its round limit, a per-project maximum caps the total, and the numbers are final once the delivery settles. There is no seat, no module and no renewal date; there is a project that either delivers or does not.

Governance is where the two answer different questions. SPSS's page describes licensing routes rather than data handling, and our capture does not set out a per-project sandbox, a training-use policy or a retention period, so we make no claim. For most institutions those terms live in the licence and the data-processing agreement, which is the correct place to look. HumanPen publishes them on the product page: transport encryption, an isolated sandbox per project, no sharing across users, no use for training, deletion at any time, and retention for 6 months after the last activity with a reminder 3 days before expiry. Its developer page adds an API at https://api.humanpen.net/v1, plan confirmation carrying a spec_version, signed short-lived delivery links for the zip, and webhooks holding only the project and its state.

When to choose which

Choose SPSS if the work is continuous, shared and taught: a department where several people run analyses every week, where the method set is stable, where students need to learn a package they will meet in industry, and where a campus licence already exists. The per-user model is then efficient, the module ladder maps onto real needs, and the free trial lets a new group test the fit before a purchase order.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if the work is discrete and documentary: one dataset, one specified analysis, one report that has to be defensible, and nobody on the team who wants to spend a fortnight in a statistics package. The confirmed plan, the second-AI review, the 300 dpi figures, the three-line tables and the delivered code are built for that shape of job.

Use both if your group has a permanent analytical function and a variable writing load. Keep SPSS seats for the people who analyse routinely and need the platform's breadth; commission the report, figures and tables as separate projects, accepting that the execution is Python or R and that any discrepancy between the two runs has to be reconciled on purpose. Treating them as one workflow is how numbers quietly diverge.
