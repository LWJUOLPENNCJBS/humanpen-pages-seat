Consensus vs HumanPen Start Project: Cited Answers About the Literature vs Analysis of Your Own Data

Short answer

Choose Consensus when you want a fast, cited answer to a question that the published literature can settle. Its pricing page describes the paid product in one line — "Cited AI analysis of peer-reviewed research" — and that is the honest scope of the offer: ask a question, get an answer with the papers attached. The free tier keeps "basic paper search" at $0, and the paid tiers exist to remove the message limit and unlock the analysis.

Choose HumanPen Start Project when the question can only be answered by running numbers on a file you already have. You attach Excel, CSV, Word, PDF or zip up to 5 GB total, confirm a plan, Python or R runs in an isolated sandbox, and the delivery is a .docx report with three-line tables, 300 dpi figures, a .pptx with speaker notes and the code — reviewed by a second AI before it reaches you.

The distinction is not quality, it is direction. Consensus reads outward into the literature. Start Project reads inward into your dataset. A literature answer and a computed result are different artefacts, and confusing them is the most common way a methods section gets into trouble.

Where the workflows actually diverge

1. A conversation versus a contract

Consensus is priced by subscription tier and message allowance: $12/mo on Pro for unlimited Pro messages, $45/mo on Deep for people conducting frequent literature reviews. That pricing implies a dialogue: you ask, the system answers with citations, you ask again.

Start Project works in projects, not in messages, and is gated by a plan. Describe the task, attach files, the system confirms requirements and proposes a plan, and "Plan first, start when you confirm". The five stages are Describe, Plan, In progress, In review, Delivered. There is no message budget to ration, and no answer until you have approved a scope.

That gate is the substantive difference. A chat answer is provisional by design; a confirmed plan is a commitment, and the deliverable is judged against the scope the system said it would compute.

2. What "analysis" means on each side

On Consensus, "analysis" means synthesis of published findings: the product reads peer-reviewed research and produces a cited answer. That is genuinely useful and, for a clinical question with an established evidence base, often faster than anything else.

On Start Project, "analysis" means estimation. The published example is a secondary analysis of the International Stroke Trial: full analysis set of 19,435 patients, primary endpoint death or dependency at 6 months, about 150 missing outcomes handled with a sensitivity analysis, aspirin group 62.2% versus control 63.5%, OR 0.95 (0.89–1.00), P = 0.072. The review panel checks that the analysis populations add up, that a clean rerun reproduces the results exactly, and that all 11 references were found one by one.

Notice that the second example produces a number that exists nowhere in the literature until the run finishes. No amount of citation can supply it. That is the dividing line.

3. The citation question is not the same on both sides

Both products care about citations, but they mean different things by the word. For Consensus, citation is the value proposition: the answer is only as good as the papers under it, so the product is built to show them.

For Start Project, citation is one of several checks. The page shows a PubMed search for the aspirin trials and both DOIs verified, then a written standard where "Numbers and citations kept exactly" — the before/after sample rewrites a stiff sentence about the 62.2% and 63.5% figures without touching either number, producing "At 6 months, 62.2% of the aspirin group and 63.5% of the control group were dead or dependent; the difference was not significant (P = 0.072)."

The rewrite is the interesting part. It is a de-AI-tone pass that keeps digits and references fixed while removing stock phrasing. If you have ever had a language model "helpfully" round a percentage, you know why the constraint matters.

Uploads and what you actually receive

Consensus's public page describes a search-and-analysis product. It does not describe uploading a spreadsheet, a cohort extract or a folder of PDFs for computation, and it does not itemise export formats. If your workflow needs a specific file type at the end, that is a question for their support rather than an assumption to make from a comparison article.

Start Project's intake is explicit: Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip, up to 5 GB total, with the caveat that Wind and CNKI data must be exported first and GPU training is not yet supported. The delivery is a folder — the report, the figures at 300 dpi to journal standards, the three-line tables with captions, the slides with speaker notes, and the code.

Revisions are handled inside the project: you request a change, the scope is explained, work starts after you confirm, a new version is delivered and past versions are kept. For anything that will be examined by a committee, version history is not a convenience; it is the audit trail.

Pricing and governance

Consensus runs a clean three-tier ladder. Free is $0/mo with basic paper search and no AI analysis. Pro is $12/mo or $144 annually and adds unlimited Pro messages plus cited AI analysis. Deep is $45/mo or $540 annually, aimed at "researchers or clinicians conducting frequent Literature Reviews". The page also advertises up to 40% off for students, faculty with a valid school email, and US HCPs with a valid NPI number — the most concrete pricing signal on the page for anyone in a university or a clinic.

What the page does not do is itemise the differences between Pro and Deep beyond those two sentences, so we do not invent a feature matrix for them. The same restraint applies to governance: the page we examined does not describe data handling, training policy or retention, so this article asserts none of those things about Consensus.

Start Project's positions are stated on its own page: encrypted in transit, each project in an isolated sandbox, no sharing across users, no use for training, deletion at any time, and 6 months of retention after the last activity with a reminder 3 days before expiry. Those are claims by the vendor, verifiable on the page, and they are the kind of claim a data-management plan asks you to write down.

The two products also meter different things: a $12/mo message allowance and a per-project scope confirmation are not convertible, so any "cheaper" verdict here would be arithmetic theatre. We are not offering one.

When to choose which

Choose Consensus if:

  • The question is answerable from published evidence and you need the answer with citations today.
  • You are a student or clinician who qualifies for the 40% discount and wants a low-commitment entry point.
  • You are checking whether a claim is supported before you build anything on top of it.
  • Your output is a reading of the literature, not an estimate from a dataset.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if:

  • You have data — a survey file, a cohort extract, trial records, a panel — and the missing step is the statistics.
  • The analysis was done in SPSS or Stata and needs to be redone in Python or R with the code retained.
  • The deliverable is a report with three-line tables, 300 dpi figures, methods-consistent prose and slides, reviewed before it reaches you.
  • You expect revisions and want each one scoped, confirmed and versioned.

Use both if your project starts with a claim and ends with a test. Use Consensus to establish what is already known and to get citable support for the gap, then bring the dataset into Start Project and let Python or R produce the estimate that fills it. The first step produces sentences with references; the second produces a table with a rerun behind it. A discussion section needs both, and they arrive in that order.

FAQ

Is Consensus free? Yes, at $0/mo, with basic paper search and no AI analysis. Pro at $12/mo or $144 annually adds unlimited Pro messages and cited AI analysis of peer-reviewed research. Deep is $45/mo or $540 annually for researchers or clinicians conducting frequent literature reviews.

Does Consensus analyse my own dataset? The public page we examined describes search and cited analysis of peer-reviewed research. It does not describe uploading a dataset for statistical computation. If your task is a regression on your own file, that is a different tool category.

Is there a student discount for Consensus? Yes. Students and faculty with a valid school email, and US HCPs with a valid NPI number, can get up to 40% off a subscription according to the pricing page.

Can HumanPen Start Project answer a literature question? Partly. The page shows a PubMed search with both DOIs checked and lists literature review and evidence building as a use case, and the writing pass keeps numbers and citations exactly as given. But it does not publish a screening scale, so for "what does the field say" the faster answer is usually a dedicated literature product.
