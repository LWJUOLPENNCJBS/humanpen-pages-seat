RStudio vs HumanPen Start Project: You Write the R, or You Approve the Plan

RStudio is the right choice when the analysis is yours to write. It is an IDE for R, the Open Source Edition is free under an AGPL v3 licence, an RStudio Desktop Pro licence is listed at $1204.00 per year with a commercial licence and priority support, and Posit AI can be added to any edition for $20/month with unlimited Next Edit Suggestions and Posit Assistant credits, a free trial and no credit card. You stay in the editor, in the session, in the code.

HumanPen Start Project is the right choice when the deliverable is a document rather than a session. You describe the study, attach the data, and the project returns a plan; work starts only when you confirm it. The analysis runs in Python or R, a second AI reviews the result before delivery, and what comes back is a .docx report, 300 dpi figures, three-line tables, a .pptx with speaker notes and the code.

The distinction is not "free versus paid" — both have a free entry point, and the project charges by usage rather than by seat. It is who writes the code. RStudio gives one person a very good place to write it; HumanPen takes a specification and returns the artefact with the script attached. The first is a craft tool, the second a commissioning arrangement.

Two workflows that look similar from a distance

Both involve R, and that is roughly where the resemblance stops.

RStudio's loop is edit, run, inspect, repeat: load the data into the session, write the model, look at the output, change the model. It is fast because it is local and because you decide what to try next. Nothing in the loop is a handover — the person who specifies the analysis and the person who executes it are the same person, which is why the tool can stay silent about what the analysis should be.

HumanPen's loop is describe, plan, confirm, run, review, deliver. The five stages are visible on the page — Describe, Plan, In progress, In review, Delivered — and the governing rule is "Plan first, start when you confirm". Before any compute is spent you receive a specification you can argue with: population, endpoint, model, what happens to missing data, which references will be checked.

That changes what an error costs. In RStudio it costs your afternoon: rerun the chunk and look again. In a commissioned project an error caught at the plan stage costs a message, and the review step exists to catch what a single pass misses. The page names four checks: the primary endpoint matches at 62.2% against 63.5% with P = 0.072, every analysis population adds up, a clean rerun reproduces the numbers exactly, and all 11 references were found, one by one.

If you know the model you want, the first loop is shorter. If the model is the uncertainty, the second forces it into writing before it becomes a table.

Where the AI sits in each product

RStudio's AI is an assistant inside the editor. Posit AI is priced at $20/month, can be added to any edition, and includes unlimited Next Edit Suggestions and Posit Assistant credits, with a free trial that requires no credit card. The page describes Posit Assistant as living inside RStudio with access to your loaded packages, variables and data frames, answering from your real data rather than generic assumptions.

The same page carries a data statement specific to that product: "Zero data retention: your code and data are never stored or used for training." We quote it as the Posit Assistant description, not as a claim about every Posit product.

HumanPen's AI is not in an editor at all. It sits in the delivery path: the plan is generated first, the run executes in the project's sandbox, and a second AI reviews the output before you see it. That review is not a style pass — it reconciles the numbers the text claims against the numbers the analysis produced, and reports "all 11 references found, one by one" rather than assuming the bibliography is intact.

There is also a writing stage with its own constraint: a de-AI-tone pass that leaves numbers and citations exactly as they are. The public before-and-after example keeps the same percentages and the same P value, rephrased into a shorter sentence rather than rounded or restated — which matters, because the usual failure of machine-written results sections is drift in the digits.

The practical reading: RStudio's AI cuts keystrokes; HumanPen's review cuts the chance a delivered document contains a number the analysis did not produce.

Uploads, limits and what a finished job looks like

RStudio has no upload envelope: the data is already on your machine, and the page we examined does not state a size ceiling for the desktop IDE.

HumanPen's intake is stated precisely: Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip, up to 5 GB total — a project-sized envelope, enough for a dataset, a codebook and a protocol.

The engines are stated too: analysis runs in Python or R; work that began in SPSS or Stata is redone in Python or R rather than run in the original package; Wind and CNKI data must be exported first; GPU training is not supported. Those four lines tell you quickly whether a project fits. A deep-learning pipeline does not; a regression on a cohort extract does.

The output is the part that differs most from an IDE. From RStudio you get the session and the script; the document is a separate act of rendering, with the figure sizes and table styles you set. From HumanPen you get the document: a .docx report, charts at 300 dpi to journal standard, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes for the lab meeting, and the code.

Revisions follow the plan's logic: scope the change, confirm, receive a new version with the old one kept — the model you want when a co-author asks what changed between drafts.

Price and governance

RStudio's price list is unusually legible. The Open Source Edition is free, and the page is explicit that RStudio will remain free and that using it without AI costs nothing. RStudio Desktop Pro is $1204.00 per year, buying a commercial licence for organisations that cannot use AGPL software plus priority email support with an 8-hour response during business hours ET. Posit AI is a separate $20/month, addable to any edition, with a free trial and no credit card.

HumanPen does not sell seats. Billing is metered by actual usage, failed tasks are not billed, and the plan itself carries no price up front — scoping typically costs tens of credits, and the figure becomes concrete once a plan exists. A round limit stops the project and asks before continuing; a per-project credit ceiling caps it. When billing.settled is true, the number is final.

Governance diverges architecturally rather than contractually. RStudio runs on your machine; the data is where you put it, and the licence means the terms cannot be withdrawn. HumanPen runs in a sandbox on someone else's infrastructure, and the protections are the ones written down: encryption in transit, one isolated sandbox per project, no sharing across users, no use for training, deletion at any time, and retention of six months after the last activity with a reminder three days before expiry.

Neither is automatically safer. The first keeps custody with you, and the burden of backups and access control too; the second replaces custody with a commitment you read before uploading — the thing to read carefully if the data is clinical.

When to choose which

Choose RStudio if you are the analyst and want to stay the analyst. A free AGPL v3 edition with no seat cost is a remarkable starting position; the Pro licence at $1204.00 per year adds a commercial licence and an 8-hour support response; $20/month for Posit AI is a modest increment. Where the code is the intellectual contribution, writing it is not overhead.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if the code is a means and the paper is the end. A methods and results section that has to survive review, a reproduction of a published table, a clinical report with 300 dpi figures — these are delivery problems, and the plan-first, review-before-delivery structure is built for them.

Use both if you have a discovery phase and a delivery phase. Prototype in RStudio: try three specifications, look at the residuals, decide what the paper will claim. Then write that decision into a brief, confirm the plan, and take delivery of the report, figures, tables and code. The IDE answers "what is in this data"; the project answers "what does the paper say, and can someone rerun it".

The mistake to avoid is treating a script as a manuscript: a script that runs is not a results section, and a session that produced a figure is not a figure legend.
