Three days before a grant deadline: what to hand to HumanPen Start Project, and what not to

The last three days of a grant call are not a writing problem. They are a sequencing problem: the budget justification needs the work packages, the work packages need the preliminary results, the preliminary results need a figure that does not yet exist, and the portal closes at a fixed time regardless of which of those is unfinished.

This is the sequence we would use to get a draft application and its supporting analysis to a submittable state in three days, with HumanPen Start Project doing the analysis and the document production. It assumes you already know the call's requirements. If you do not, stop and read them first — no amount of production capacity fixes an ineligible application.

First day, morning: write the scope note, not the proposal

One page, four headings: what the project will establish, how it is organised into work packages, what each package delivers, and what evidence already exists that the approach is feasible. The last heading is the one that decides whether the application is credible, and it is the one that most applications fill with adjectives.

Write it before you write prose. Every later section is an expansion of one of those four headings, and a scope note that cannot be written is a proposal that cannot be written either.

First day, afternoon: build one preliminary result properly

A grant application does not need many analyses. It needs one result that is correct, well presented and clearly connected to the aim. Choose the analysis that most directly demonstrates feasibility, and produce it to publication standard: a table, a figure and one paragraph.

This is the job to hand to HumanPen Start Project first, because it has the tightest dependency chain. Attach the dataset, describe the comparison, name the endpoint, and state the deliverable: a report with a three-line table, a figure at 300 dpi and the code. The public example shows the level of specificity to expect — a full analysis set of 19,435 patients, a primary endpoint of death or dependency at six months, about 150 missing outcomes handled in a sensitivity analysis, and a review that checks the endpoint matches (62.2% against 63.5%, P = 0.072) before delivery.

Two practical notes. Upload files up to 5 GB total, so send the raw exports and the codebook rather than a hand-merged spreadsheet. And read the plan before you approve it: if the plan names a different population from the one in your aim, that is a correction to make now, not after delivery.

Second day, morning: write the approach around the numbers

Now write the approach and feasibility sections, using the preliminary result as the anchor. Every claim about feasibility should point at the table or the figure. Where the analysis does not yet support a claim, write the plan to test it rather than the claim itself.

Keep the numbers exactly as delivered. HumanPen Start Project's writing pass preserves numbers and citations while removing stock phrasing; if a rewrite changes a number, reject the rewrite.

Second day, afternoon: make the budget explain the work packages

The budget justification is a translation of the work packages into effort, consumables and time. If a line item cannot be traced to a package, either the package is missing or the line is. Reviewers notice both.

Do not ask the analysis to produce a budget. Budgets are institutional documents with rules that vary by funder; the useful contribution is a work package list with effort estimates that a research office can price.

Third day, morning: read against the criteria, not against your intentions

Print the review criteria and read the draft with one question per criterion: where in this document is that criterion addressed? Mark the paragraph. Criteria with no paragraph are the revisions that matter.

Then check the four things that reviewers can verify mechanically: does the preliminary table's N match the text, is the endpoint defined once, do the references resolve, are the figures at print resolution. Those are the same four checks HumanPen Start Project reports as its independent review, and they are worth re-running on the assembled document.

Third day, afternoon: format, then stop

Page limits, margins, font, reference style, required sections, eligibility statements, attachments. Convert to the portal's required format and read the generated file, not the source.

Two failure modes to avoid in the final hours: adding a new analysis (it will not be reviewed and may contradict the narrative), and letting a co-author rewrite the aims (the work packages no longer match).

What to do if the preliminary analysis is not ready

Use the strongest result you already have, stated with its limitations, and describe the planned analysis in the approach. Reviewers assess feasibility, and a documented plan with an honest preliminary result is more credible than an over-claimed figure produced under time pressure.

If a required analysis will not finish, say so in the timeline section rather than in the results. A work package with a stated risk reads as competence.

The one thing to cut when time runs out

If the third day arrives with work outstanding, cut the section that no review criterion refers to. Most drafts contain one: a background paragraph on a neighbouring field, a methods detail that belongs in a supplementary file, an extra figure that duplicates a table.

What must not be cut is the link between the aim and the preliminary result. That link is what reviewers use to judge feasibility, and a proposal with a weaker narrative but an intact evidence chain outscores one with a polished narrative and a gap where the evidence should be.

Write that link as three sentences — the aim, the result that supports it, and the work package that would extend it — and keep them outside the draft. When a co-author asks for a change on the last afternoon, those three sentences are what you check the change against.

Frequently asked questions

Can a report be produced in a day?

A single, well-specified analysis with a table and a figure often can, because the plan step front-loads the ambiguity. The constraint is your availability to confirm the plan, not the computing.

What if the funder requires a specific template?

Supply the template's headings in the brief and ask for the narrative in those sections. Formatting a document into a funder template after the fact is a reliable way to lose a day.

Should the application mention that analysis support was used?

Follow the funder's and your institution's disclosure rules on contributors and on tool use, and list contributions accurately in whatever section they specify.

How do I keep the numbers consistent between the draft and the final report?

Keep one source of truth: the delivered report. Copy numbers from it into the narrative, never retype them from memory, and do a final grep of the document for every number that appears in a table.
