Wordtune vs HumanPen Condense: Rewriting Sentences vs Meeting a Word Budget

Short answer

Choose Wordtune when the sentence is the unit of work. Its public page lists paraphrase, rewrite, "read and summarize", AI writing, a grammar checker and "Humanize AI", and it is free to start with no credit card required. That combination fits the moment when a paragraph is correct but clumsy, when a claim needs restating in plainer words, when you want a second phrasing of a sentence you have already decided to keep. It is a writing assistant that works close to the cursor.

Choose HumanPen Condense when the number is the unit of work. It reduces a document to a target word count while preserving meaning, structure and citations. You set an overall word budget, or a separate target per selected passage. It keeps facts, numbers, citations and structure, and trims wordiness and repetition. Input is a .docx; output is an editable DOCX with the original styles and layout, at 20 credits per 1,000 words.

The difference is not quality; it is what the instruction is. "Make this sentence better" and "make this document fit the limit without losing a citation" are different requests, and a tool built around the first does not become the second by being asked politely.

Where the two tools actually diverge

1. Sentence-level craft versus document-level arithmetic

A paraphrase engine optimises locally. Given a sentence, it produces a sentence of roughly similar length that says the same thing differently, and that is the correct behaviour for its job: the surrounding paragraph stays put, the rhythm improves, nothing downstream moves.

Length limits are global. If a chapter is over the limit, the surplus has to come from somewhere, and the choice of where is the actual work. Cutting evenly by ratio ruins a methods section that was already tight. Cutting only the conclusion may break the argument. Wordtune's page describes features that act on text; it does not describe a target word count you set and a tool that allocates the cut across a document.

Condense is built around that allocation. The overall budget is one option; the per-passage target is the more interesting one, because it lets you say "this section loses length, that one loses nothing". The page states what must survive that process: facts, numbers, citations, structure.

2. Summarising produces a reading; condensing produces a submission

Wordtune lists "read and summarize" among its features, alongside paraphrase and rewrite. Summarising is a reading aid: you feed it text, it gives you the gist. The gist is not a document anyone grades, reviews or publishes, and it is not meant to be — you read the summary to decide whether to read the text.

Condense's output is the text. Same file, same styles, same layout, fewer words, and the citations still pointing at the same sources in the same order. The page does not describe producing a digest or a set of notes; it describes editing the document you gave it.

This is the distinction worth keeping straight: a summary replaces your document with a shorter reading of it, while Condense is a deliverable of your document at a shorter length for a word limit. If what you hand over must open in Word and look like your manuscript, only the second shape of output qualifies.

3. Where the work sits in the timeline

Wordtune's feature list reads like it belongs during drafting: paraphrase while writing, rewrite a flagged sentence, check grammar, humanise a passage. The value accumulates across many small decisions made in the flow of writing, and the page's "Free to start, no credit card required" framing matches that low-commitment, always-open posture.

Condense belongs after the draft stops changing, when the artefact is fixed and only its size is contested. Its contract is a file rather than a text box: .docx in, editable DOCX out, 7-day file retention, 20 credits per 1,000 words, a job record that stays after the files are removed.

Neither placement is better. A writing assistant used at submission time is late; a word-budget reducer used mid-draft is premature, because you would be condensing text you are about to rewrite anyway. The pages describe different moments, and the honest advice is to respect the moment you are in.

When to choose which (or use both)

Choose Wordtune if the draft is still moving, if the problem is phrasing rather than extent, or if you want grammar and rewording handled in the same place you write. Its free-to-start entry with no credit card required also makes it reasonable to try on a single paragraph before committing to any workflow.

Choose HumanPen Condense if a limit has been imposed and the content is settled: an abstract that must fit, a section a co-author wants shortened, a report that must not lose a figure or a reference. Choose it when the deliverable has to remain a .docx that opens with its heading styles and layout intact.

Use both, in that order. Rewrite and tighten sentences while drafting; stop; then condense the finished file to the number the venue enforces.
