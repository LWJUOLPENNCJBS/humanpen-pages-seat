Wordvice vs HumanPen Start Project: A Human Editor vs a Project That Runs the Numbers

Wordvice is the better choice when the manuscript is finished and the remaining problem is English. It is a human editing service, priced by the word: proofreading runs $0.023 to $0.044 per word, or around $23 to $44 per 1,000 words. You are buying an editor's attention on prose you have already written, and the Premium tier adds a manuscript review report plus free re-editing for 365 days on Academic work, or 14 days on Admissions.

HumanPen Start Project is the better choice when the manuscript is not finished because the analysis behind it has not been done. You describe the task and attach the data, receive a plan, confirm it, and the project runs the analysis in Python or R, puts the result through a second AI review, and delivers a .docx report, 300 dpi figures, three-line tables, a .pptx with speaker notes and the code.

These are not two versions of the same purchase. One is labour applied to a sentence; the other is production applied to a dataset. Comparing their prices per unit is meaningless, because the units differ.

These are not the same kind of product

Most "X vs Y" comparisons in this category are feature lists between two self-serve tools. This one is not. Wordvice sells a service: a document goes in, an editor works on it, a document comes back. HumanPen Start Project sells an outcome: a brief goes in, a plan comes back, and after your confirmation an analysis and its write-up come back.

That difference changes what "better" means.

With an editing service, the quality question is about the person who received your file: the variance is editorial, and the recourse is a re-edit. With a project service, the question is the chain of custody behind a number — which dataset, which population, which model, which code, which version — and the recourse is a reproducible rerun.

Neither of those is a superior form of assurance. They are assurances about different objects. A brilliant editor can make a wrong odds ratio read beautifully, and a perfect pipeline will not fix a paragraph that reads like a translation.

What each one is accountable for

This is the most practical distinction in the whole comparison, so it is worth stating plainly.

Wordvice's pages describe proofreading and editing of a manuscript you supply: a human editor works on the English. The Premium tier's manuscript review report extends that into commentary on the manuscript as a piece of writing, and the re-edit terms extend it in time. Everything in that list is about the text. The pages we examined do not describe computing statistics from a dataset, and they do not describe delivering analysis code as part of the order.

HumanPen Start Project's pages describe the opposite half. A project opens with the brief and the files, and the first output is a plan rather than prose. The public example is a secondary analysis of the International Stroke Trial: a full analysis set of 19,435 patients, a primary endpoint of death or dependency at 6 months, about 150 missing outcomes handled with a sensitivity analysis, 62.2% in the aspirin group against 63.5% in the control group, OR 0.95 (0.89–1.00), P = 0.072. The references were retrieved through PubMed with every DOI checked, and the review step reports that all 11 references were found one by one.

Notice what is absent from that list: any claim about how the sentences sound. HumanPen's writing step keeps numbers and citations exactly as they are, with a de-AI-tone pass that removes stock phrasing — a narrower brief than an editor's, and the page does not pretend otherwise.

So the honest division is: an editor is accountable for how your argument reads, and a project is accountable for whether the numbers in it are right and can be regenerated. Most papers need both, at different times.

The money, read as a rate

Wordvice's pricing is legible in a way most subscription software is not. The rate is $0.023 to $0.044 per word, which the page also expresses as roughly $23 to $44 per 1,000 words. Because it is per word, the cost scales with the length of the document rather than with the calendar.

For a single paper that is easy to reason about: you know the word count before you order. For a thesis, or a lab that sends every manuscript out, the bill grows with output rather than staying flat — either the fairest arrangement or an uncomfortable one, depending on how much you publish.

HumanPen sells credits rather than subscription seats, so this comparison quotes no per-month figure for it. The unit of sale is a scoped project, not a rate card. What the page does specify is the boundary a quote would respect — 5 GB total intake, Python or R, SPSS and Stata work redone in Python or R, Wind and CNKI data exported first, no GPU training.

One warning about comparing the two on cost. The temptation is to convert an editing rate into a project budget and declare a winner. A per-word fee buys a pass over prose that exists; a project buys the production of the prose and its evidence. If you have no results yet, the editing rate is not a cheaper option — it is an option for a later stage of the same paper.

Guarantees, re-edits and retention

Read the assurance language closely, because the two products assure different things and one of them is easy to misread.

Wordvice's home page carries the line "100% Satisfaction Guaranteed", and it sits directly beside the Premium tier's manuscript review report and free re-editing terms. That is Wordvice's own service term, describing Wordvice's own remedy, and we quote it as such. It is not a claim we make, and it is not a claim about a submission outcome. The same page also reports a 91%+ repeat order rate, which is a loyalty measure rather than a quality score — worth noting, because the two get conflated in summaries of this category.

The re-edit terms are the concrete part. Premium carries free re-editing for 365 days on Academic work and 14 days on Admissions; Standard carries a 30% discount on re-edits within 21 days. For a paper that goes through two review rounds, a year of re-editing is a substantial inclusion.

HumanPen's assurance mechanism is a gate rather than a guarantee. The five stages are Describe, Plan, In progress, In review and Delivered; work starts only after you confirm the plan, and before delivery a second AI reviews the result. The example shows that review as a checklist — endpoint matches, populations add up, a clean rerun reproduces the results, all eleven references found — printed as "Review passed (round 1)". A named check with a stated result is auditable in a way a satisfaction promise is not.

Retention closes the loop. HumanPen keeps a project for 6 months after the last activity, reminds you 3 days before expiry and lets you delete it at any time; revisions are scoped and confirmed before work starts, a new version is delivered and the old one kept. For a paper queried during a slow review cycle, that is the difference between reopening a project and rebuilding one.

When to choose which

Choose Wordvice if the manuscript exists, the analysis is settled, and the gap is English. A per-word rate is predictable, the Premium tier's manuscript review report gives you a second reader as well as a copy-edit, and a year of free re-editing on Academic work covers the resubmission cycle. If your supervisor's note says "the English needs work before this goes anywhere", this is the service that note is asking for.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if the gap is upstream: the dataset has not been analysed, the table does not exist, the figure has to be redrawn at 300 dpi, or a published result has to be reproduced before you can argue with it. The plan checkpoint, the second-AI review, the delivered code and the 6-month retention are all aimed at the moment a reviewer asks how a number was produced. The 5 GB total intake suits a dataset plus its documentation rather than a single manuscript.

Use both if you are producing a paper from raw data. Run the analysis in HumanPen Start Project, confirm the plan, take delivery of the report, figures, tables and code, then send the manuscript to Wordvice for a human language pass with a year of re-editing behind it. Both ends speak .docx: one buys correctness of the evidence, the other readability of the argument.

The mismatch to avoid is ordering an edit on a draft whose results will change: editing a section about to be rerun is the most expensive way to write a second draft.
