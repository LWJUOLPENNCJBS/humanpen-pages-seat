GraphPad Prism vs HumanPen Start Project: A Trial You Drive vs a Plan You Confirm

Prism is the stronger choice when the statistics belong in your hands and the figure has to look right before it goes anywhere. GraphPad's public page puts a free trial at the centre of the decision — "No commitment or credit card required" — which is the right offer for software you have to feel before you can judge it: you bring your own data, run the analysis, style the graph, and find out within a week whether the workflow suits the way your lab thinks.

HumanPen Start Project is the stronger choice when the deliverable is a document rather than a session. You describe the task and attach the data, the system returns a plan, and nothing runs until you confirm it. Then the analysis is carried out in Python or R, a second AI reviews it, and what comes back is a .docx report with 300 dpi figures, three-line tables and captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code that produced every number.

The honest difference is one of kind, not of quality. Prism is a tool you operate; HumanPen is a project you commission. One gives you a workspace and a learning curve; the other gives you a manuscript-ready file and a set of questions you have to answer before it starts. This article compares the two workflows, and it deliberately does not compare their prices: the Prism pricing page returned a 404 to our capture, so there is no number on our side of the table to argue about.

How the workflows differ

1. A trial is an evaluation instrument; a plan is a specification

A free trial with no commitment and no card is a genuinely good commercial design, because it moves the risk to the vendor. You can spend a week testing whether the software's defaults match your field's conventions before you commit a budget line. That is worth more than a feature table: the question "can I get this graph to look the way my journal wants it" is answered by trying, not by reading.

A confirmed plan is a different instrument. It does not tell you whether you will enjoy the work; it tells you what the work will be. In the public example on the HumanPen page — a secondary analysis of the International Stroke Trial — the plan restates the analysis set at 19,435 patients, the primary endpoint of death or dependency at 6 months, and a sensitivity analysis for about 150 missing outcomes, and waits. Nothing is computed until someone agrees that this is the right question.

One instrument reduces the risk of buying the wrong software. The other reduces the risk of answering the wrong question. Most research groups need both at different moments, and confusing them is how people end up with a beautiful figure of the wrong endpoint.

2. Where the analysis actually happens

Prism's model, as far as the public page we examined describes it, is a researcher driving an application: you supply the data, choose the analysis, and shape the figure. That is a coherent design for experimental science, where the analyst is the person who ran the experiment and the judgement calls are made by looking at the data. The page we captured does not describe hosted execution of an analysis on a vendor's infrastructure, and it does not describe a delivered code artefact; we therefore do not characterise either way.

HumanPen's model is the reverse. There is no application to learn. The analysis runs in Python or R in an isolated sandbox, and what crosses back to you is a finished document plus the code. That is a worse fit for exploratory work — you cannot nudge a parameter and watch a curve move — and a better fit for work that has already been specified and now has to be produced, checked and handed over.

The practical test is simple. If you would be unhappy not seeing the intermediate plots, you want the first model. If you would be unhappy being the person who has to produce the intermediate plots, you want the second.

3. Three kinds of reproducibility, only one of which is a document

Reproducibility gets used for three different things: the same numbers, the same figure, and the same decision. A document can carry the first two. The third lives in the reasoning, and it is the hardest to hand over.

HumanPen states four checks before delivery, and three of them are about numbers rather than prose: the primary endpoint matches (62.2% vs 63.5%, P = 0.072 in the example), every analysis population adds up, and a clean rerun reproduces the results exactly. The fourth is bibliographic — all 11 references found, one by one. Alongside the report you receive the code, which is what makes a rerun possible for someone who was not in the room.

Whether Prism produces an equivalent artefact is not something the page we examined addresses; its public material describes the analysis and the graphs, not a handover package. That is not a criticism, it is a category difference: software returns a workspace, and a service returns a dossier.

Uploading data and what comes back

The Prism page we captured does not state a data-intake ceiling, a file-size limit or a list of accepted formats, so we do not assert any. What it does state is the trial terms, which is what most people need before a first evaluation: no commitment and no credit card.

HumanPen states its intake in one line: Excel, CSV, Word, PDF and zip, up to 5 GB total. It also states its boundaries, which is rarer and more useful: analysis runs on Python or R, analyses originally done in SPSS or Stata are redone in Python or R, Wind and CNKI data must be exported first, and GPU training is not yet supported. A project that is one dataset, its codebook and a folder of PDFs fits comfortably; a nightly stream of instrument output does not.

The delivery is specified down to the artefact: a .docx report, figures at 300 dpi to journal standards, three-line tables with captions, a .pptx with speaker notes, and the code. For a thesis chapter or a submission, that is closer to the thing you actually have to hand in than a graph file is.

Price shape and governance

We do not quote a Prism price. The pricing URL returned a 404 to our capture on 24 November 2026, so any figure in this article would be invention, and a wrong number in a comparison post is worse than a missing one. Buyers should read GraphPad's own commercial pages on the day they buy, and should read the trial terms first — "No commitment or credit card required" is a clear statement that evaluation costs nothing.

HumanPen's price shape is a meter rather than a list. Work is billed in real time by what it consumes, failed tasks are not charged, and a plan carries no price up front: scoping a request is charged, usually a few dozen credits, and the plan you confirm is what the run is measured against. A round stops and asks once it hits its limit, a per-project ceiling caps the total, and the figures are final when the delivery settles. For a group that has to justify a spend before it happens, that is a different conversation from a licence quote — and, to be fair, a less comfortable one for anyone who wants a number before they start.

Governance is where the two are easiest to compare, because HumanPen publishes specifics and the Prism page we examined does not. HumanPen states transport encryption, an isolated sandbox per project, no sharing across users, no use for training, deletion at any time, and retention for 6 months after the last activity with a reminder 3 days before expiry. The developer page adds an API at https://api.humanpen.net/v1, a plan confirmation carrying a spec_version, signed short-lived delivery links, and webhooks holding only the project and its state. Our capture of GraphPad's public material does not describe a data-retention period or a training-use policy, so we make no claim in either direction; if those terms decide your purchase, ask the vendor and read the licence.

When to choose which

Choose Prism if the statistics are part of the experiment: dose-response work, curve fitting you want to inspect visually, figures that will be restyled for three journals, or a lab that teaches analysis and needs students to see each step. The free trial with no commitment and no card makes the evaluation cheap, and operating the software yourself is the point rather than an overhead.

Choose HumanPen Start Project if the endpoint is a document: a paper's methods and results, a clinical report, a grant's preliminary analysis, a set of published results you need reproduced with notes on the differences. The confirmed plan, the second-AI review, the 300 dpi figures, the three-line tables and the delivered code are aimed at the reader six weeks later.

Use both if your work has an exploratory front and a documentary back. Fit and look in Prism, where the feedback is instant, settle the specification, then hand the dataset and the settled plan to HumanPen and take the report, figures, tables and code that go into the submission. Keep the Prism project as your working record and the .docx and code as the defensible one.

The mismatch to avoid is commissioning a report when you have not yet decided what to measure, or buying a workspace when what you owe your co-authors is a document.

Can Prism deliver a Word report with three-line tables and the code? Does HumanPen Start Project replace Prism for routine lab analysis? For a specified analysis that ends in a document, it can carry the work end to end.
