Mendeley vs HumanPen Citation Fix: A Library You Cite From vs a Pass That Conforms the File

Short answer

Choose Mendeley when the work is upstream of the sentence: when you have forty PDFs to file, highlight, and annotate, when you want a single library you can cite from over several projects, and when the citation is inserted as you type so it stays linked to the source record. Mendeley's public page calls itself "the free all-in-one reference manager" and describes adding PDFs, organising with collections and tags, reading and annotating, cite-while-you-write, an AI research companion called LeapSpace, and free storage — with the line "Trusted by millions of researchers".

Choose HumanPen Citation Fix when the manuscript is already written and the citation style is the only thing wrong. It accepts a .docx, converts the in-text citations and the reference list to a target style — one of 12 named on the page, including APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE, GB/T 7714, Vancouver, AMA, ACS, OSCOLA, and Harvard in author-date and notes variants — and returns an editable DOCX with fonts, headings, numbering, and layout preserved. The wording is not touched. At 1,000 words for 5 credits, it is the cheapest operation HumanPen offers.

They are different jobs. Mendeley's page describes library management and cite-while-you-write; it does not describe a one-pass style conversion of a finished DOCX. HumanPen's page describes the conversion and nothing about building a library. Neither statement is a criticism — it is simply where each tool starts.

Where the two tools actually diverge

1. The reading layer is the product, or it is not there at all

Mendeley's public page is mostly about custody of documents you did not write: add PDFs, file them in collections, tag them, read them, highlight them, annotate them. The citation feature sits on top of that library, which is why it can insert a citation that stays connected to a record you can reopen later.

HumanPen Citation Fix has no reading layer, and the page does not pretend otherwise. There is nothing to highlight, nothing to tag, no record to reopen. The document is the only object, and the reference list already inside it is the only data the operation reads.

This matters in a specific, practical way: the two tools have different failure modes. A library can be incomplete, and then the citation you need is missing. A finished reference list can be internally inconsistent — the same author spelled two ways, a year that disagrees with the in-text marker — and then the conversion carries that inconsistency through unless a human catches it first. Neither tool is described as fixing the other's problem.

2. Cite-while-you-write and conform-after-the-fact are not rivals

Cite-while-you-write is a bet that the citations are under your control from the first paragraph. That bet pays off in a long project: change the target journal in month six and, in principle, the citations follow, because they are still linked.

A one-pass conversion is a bet that the document arrives from elsewhere. Co-authors, translators, supervisors with tracked changes, and journal templates all produce files with citations that were never linked to anything. The .docx is the artefact; there is no field to update.

Both bets are reasonable, and they are not exclusive. A manuscript drafted with Mendeley and then reformatted for a second submission is the ordinary case, not an exotic one. HumanPen's page describes the second half of that journey and is silent on the first.

3. "Free" is doing different work in each sentence

Mendeley's page leads with "the free all-in-one reference manager" and pairs it with free storage and "Trusted by millions of researchers". The offer is a place to keep things, priced at nothing.

Citation Fix is priced per job: 1,000 words costs 5 credits, credits never expire, new accounts get 100 free credits, and failed or cancelled document jobs are never charged. The unit is a document, not a seat or a library.

So the honest comparison is not "free versus paid". It is "a free library with a storage allowance" against "a metered operation that is billed by the thousand words and voided if the job fails". If you convert one thesis once, the second model costs you almost nothing and commits you to nothing. If you maintain a library for a decade, the first model is the one built for that.

When to choose which (or use both)

Choose Mendeley if the sources are still arriving, if you annotate as you read, if you will cite the same literature across several papers, or if you want an AI companion inside the same workspace as the library.

Choose HumanPen Citation Fix if the document is done and the style is the defect. The usual triggers: a manuscript written in APA that must go out in IEEE; a thesis that has to be delivered in GB/T 7714; a resubmission to a journal that wants Vancouver; a file assembled from three co-authors where the reference list is in three conventions.

Use both. Read, annotate, and cite in Mendeley while the paper is being written. When the file is final and the destination differs from the library's output, run one Citation Fix pass over the .docx instead of reopening and re-inserting every field. The library stays the record of what you read; the DOCX becomes the record of what you submitted.

FAQ

Does Mendeley reformat a finished DOCX into another citation style in one pass? Its public page describes library management and cite-while-you-write; it does not describe a one-pass conversion of a finished DOCX from one style to another.

Does HumanPen Citation Fix build or store my library? No. It reads a .docx, converts the in-text citations and the reference list, and returns an editable DOCX with the rest of the text unchanged. Collections, tags, PDFs, and annotations are outside what the page describes.

Will my formatting survive? The page states that fonts, headings, numbering, and layout are preserved in the returned editable DOCX. Input is .docx only.

What happens to the uploaded file? Job files are kept 7 days and then removed; the job record stays. Data is encrypted in transit, never used for training, and deletable.
